# FF4 lit review — the world model as a pre-run estimator

**Why this review exists.** FF4's working thesis: *a sufficient world model of physical/expensive processes is valuable precisely where verification is expensive, slow, safety-critical, or ambiguous* — grounding an LLM's priors in a few hundred real measurement traces turns it into a usable pre-run estimator of YOUR hardware. This review surveys the adjacent literatures to find what that thesis can defensibly rest on, what the incumbent methods already achieve (the honest baseline bar), which design decisions the evidence forces, and — domain by domain — how much accurate advance prediction is actually worth. (The "language as compression of reality" section is retained as internal background on why we expect LLM priors to help at all; it is deliberately NOT the launch framing — user direction 2026-07-10.)

**Anchor:** [GPU Forecasters (arXiv:2605.31464)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31464) — LLMs as *selective surrogates* for GPU kernel runtime: forecast instead of measure, abstain when uncertain, spend real GPU time only on endorsed candidates, evaluate several times more candidates per GPU budget. FF4 is this idea generalized: any expensive process, grounded in OTel traces of real runs.

**Contents:** cross-cutting synthesis; anchor (kernel surrogates); LLM world models; language-as-compression (background); LLM numeric regression; specialized ML-systems/EDA predictors; surrogates & Bayesian optimization; physical surrogates & digital twins; experiment-outcome prediction; superoptimization & code-optimization search; database cardinality/what-if estimation; the broader map of high-value prediction targets; domain-by-domain value quantification.

## Cross-cutting synthesis — what these literatures agree on

**1. The thesis is externally validated — but text priors alone are not enough.**
DreamGym, GPU Forecasters, LLAMBO, OmniPred, and the whole physical-surrogate canon (GraphCast, Severson batteries, GNoME) validate "a model trained on records of an expensive process can stand in for it." The hard boundary: ungrounded text priors are quantitatively weak on exactly our targets (ByteSized32 shows GPT-4 at ~50% on arithmetic-driven state changes; gpuFLOPBench shows order-of-magnitude misses on implicit costs; V-JEPA ≫ text models on physics). **Grounding in measured traces is the load-bearing move in every literature** — the prior supplies the mechanism-level knowledge, the traces supply your hardware. (Why LLM priors help at all is the internal-background compression section, § "Language as compression of reality".)

**2. The incumbent bar is single-digit-percent error, so the claim must be generalization, not raw accuracy.**
Specialized predictors already hit: Vidur <9% (LLM serving), LLMCompass ~4%, GenZ ~5.8%, DNNPerf ~7% time / ~14% memory, Habitat ~12% cross-GPU, HLS-QoR ~7–20%. But every one needs per-hardware re-profiling or hand-engineered structural features, and every one degrades on unseen hardware/ops/design classes. FF4's load-bearing experiment: *match the specialized predictors on their own configs while needing only generic traces — and hold up in the held-out regimes where they break.* The strongest-claim framing survives, sharpened: beat the analytic formula and k-NN, cite the specialized SOTA honestly as the ceiling, and position transformer-math formulas as a sanity prior (or residual-modeling target), never as the headline opponent.

**3. Design decisions the literature converges on (adopted into plans/ff4.md):**
- **Single-step estimator, outcome vector + failure flags only.** Multi-step rollout is where every world model breaks (compounding error); predicting the salient delta beats reconstructing state (WMA, ByteSized32). FF4 is deliberately single-step.
- **The failure boundary is the main event.** OOM/doesn't-fit is an unknown-constraint crash region (Gelbart EIC, crash-robust BO); predict P(fail) as an explicit output, treat crashed runs as labeled points on the cliff, and evaluate boundary localization (precision/recall/F1) separately from average error. The 2026 HPO study singles out OOM-avoidance as exactly where LLM-driven search underperforms — our target is the field's named weak spot, which is both the opportunity and the risk.
- **Relative/bucketed prediction for ranking; absolute regression only where magnitude matters.** GPU Forecasters' 8-bucket relative-speedup target calibrates far better than absolute regression, and search loops consume rankings (Ansor). Report Spearman/top-k alongside normalized MAE.
- **Number formatting is a first-class decision.** Digit-by-digit or scientific-notation encoding, normalized per metric (OmniPred, LLMTime, LASER, decoding-based regression); default BPE tokenization mangles numbers.
- **Calibration comes from sampled distributions and proper scoring rules, never verbalized confidence.** RLHF models are systematically overconfident (ECE ≥ 0.30); sample numeric completions for densities (LLM Processes), score with Brier/CRPS (GPU Forecasters' GRPO rewards), report ECE and interval coverage, and make abstention/defer-to-measurement a first-class output.
- **Retrieval + rich metadata drive transfer.** OmniPred's ablation (error 0.19 → 0.44 without metadata) and the sub-linear in-context regret result: keep semantic field names in prompts, retrieve by config similarity, tune k empirically, and prefer one multi-domain WM over per-domain models (multi-task roughly halved OmniPred's error).
- **Slot into proven loops; don't replace them.** Every modern win (BOHB, ifBO, LLAMBO, Bao) keeps a classical backbone and inserts the learned model as warm-start proposer / surrogate / crash predictor. Frame the WM as the low-fidelity source in a multi-fidelity loop — the WM's correlation with ground truth *is* the fidelity metric, and it directly prices when to pay for a real run.
- **Engineer the sweep for outcome diversity.** Severson deliberately spanned 150–2,300-cycle lifetimes; space-filling seed + adaptive refinement is the settled practice; our cliff-straddling config-space rule (~⅓ failures) is the same principle.
- **Honesty rules, learned from this field's public failures.** AlphaChip (contested preprocessing/compute), A-Lab and GNoME (corrected claims), digital twins (demos outran deployments): publish the full pipeline, matched-compute baselines, per-regime skill maps rather than one number, asymmetric scoring where an optimistic miss is costlier, value stated as *real-runs-avoided*, and never call this a "digital twin" (Kritzinger taxonomy: we are a learned surrogate/pre-run estimator).

**4. Reusable assets discovered (candidate corpora/baselines, no capture cost):**
GPU Forecasters' released eval pairs + search logs + LoRA adapters; TenSet (52M tensor-program latencies); TpuGraphs; Vidur's open code + workload traces (domain-2 baseline harness); CircuitNet (>10K EDA runs with QoR labels) + HLSDataset/HLSFactory (domain 3); HPO-B / YAHPO-Gym (cheap transfer-story validation before GPU spend); MIT/Stanford/Toyota 124-cell battery set + NASA C-MAPSS/PCoE (the physical bridge); RE-Bench's 71 human score-vs-time trajectories (experiment-outcome corpus). MLE-bench/PaperBench are better used as held-out outcome-prediction test sets than as training corpora (each ground-truth point is a multi-hour agent run).

**5. The launch narrative, value-led and non-overreaching:**
Every expensive verification loop is a tax on iteration speed. Battery labs turned a 500-day charging-protocol search into 16 days by predicting months-long cycle life from the first five cycles (Severson 2019, Attia 2020); a serving-config sweep that costs 42K GPU-hours (~$218K) is answered by a simulator in an hour of CPU (Vidur 2024); kernel search finds faster kernels on the same GPU budget when an LLM forecasts which candidates deserve measurement (GPU Forecasters 2026). FF4 generalizes that move: a world model built from a few hundred real traces of YOUR runs estimates the outcome of a configuration before you commit the run — calibrated, with provenance, and with an explicit "measure this one for real" defer signal. We prove the method on compute proxies where ground truth is cheap (ML memory/OOM, vLLM serving, FPGA place-and-route — AlphaChip's own domain), then point it at physical iteration (PCB respins, bring-up logs, battery cycling), where verification is slowest, costliest, and safety-critical, and where runs-avoided is worth the most.

**6. The pattern is 40 years old and has shipped in production — with known deployment killers (wave-2 sections).**
Superoptimization has gated expensive verification behind cheap predictors since Massalin 1987 (test-vector screen → STOKE's test-case cost → PrediPrune's learned ~2× verification cut), and databases have *shipped* pre-run estimation for 25+ years (AutoAdmin what-if indexes, 1998). That history names what actually kills deployed estimators: **regressions** (one confidently-wrong prediction that makes things worse gets the estimator switched off — Ding 2019 fixed this with relative/classification framing, ~5× fewer comparison errors), **drift** (learned estimators lose to dumb baselines when they can't refresh as the world moves — "Are We Ready?" VLDB 2021), and **metric mismatch** (better average error ≠ better decisions — Han 2022's P-Error). Consequences for FF4's eval: report **decision regret** (did the estimator pick the right config?) and **regression rate** alongside error/F1, weight errors near decision boundaries, and prefer relative comparisons where the consumer is a choice.

**7. Value anchors — what accurate pre-run prediction is worth, verified (full table in the closing section).**
The strongest quantified wins: LLM serving config search **42K GPU-hours ≈ $218K → 1 CPU-hour** (Vidur, verbatim); battery protocol search **500+ days → 16 days, >30×** (Attia 2020); CI testing compute **halved at >99.9% regression recall** at Meta scale (150M test executions/day at Google); **~55% of GPU-time in a real multi-tenant cluster goes to failed/killed jobs, with GPU-OOM the largest DL-specific failure at ~8.8% of all failures** (Philly ATC'19 + ICSE'20 — the direct value anchor for our v1 domain); advanced-node mask sets **$1M–$40M** make chip respins the extreme tail. Corrections adopted: battery is >30× not ~15×; "65% of failures are OOM" is a denominator conflation — use 8.8% of all failures. PCB respin dollar figures are the weakest-sourced in the set (vendor marketing; flag them as such in the launch page).

## Anchor: selective surrogates for kernel runtime

### Anchor paper

**GPU Forecasters: Language Models as Selective Surrogates for Kernel Runtime Optimization** — Zaid Khan, Justin Chih-Yao Chen, Jaemin Cho, Elias Stengel-Eskin, Mohit Bansal. arXiv:2605.31464, 2026 (cs.LG/cs.AI). Code + data: https://github.com/codezakh/gpu-forecasters (CC BY 4.0), with datasets and LoRA adapters on HuggingFace.

*Sourcing note: the arXiv HTML/ar5iv full text failed to render (fatal conversion error), so the details below are drawn from the verified abstract page and the repository README. Claims that could not be confirmed from those two sources are flagged as inferred.*

- **Task setup.** Optimizing GPU kernels (via evolutionary search or coding agents) normally requires repeatedly compiling each candidate and measuring it on the target GPU, which dominates the cost of the search. GPU Forecasters replaces most of those measurements with an LLM that *forecasts* whether a proposed kernel will be faster, and only spends real GPU time on candidates the model endorses. It is evaluated on KernelBench models (VGG19, SwinMLP, EfficientNet, etc.) plus GPU Mode tasks (TriMul, cross-entropy, FP8 quantization).
- **How kernels/configs are represented as text.** The unit of prediction is a **parent→child kernel pair**: the search mutates an existing (parent) kernel into a candidate (child), and both kernels' source code are rendered into a surrogate prompt template. So the "config/action" is the mutated kernel source itself, presented as text alongside its parent for reference; the model reasons about the *edit* rather than an isolated program.
- **Prediction target — relative, and bucketed.** The model does **not** regress absolute runtime. It predicts the **relative speedup of child vs. parent, classified into 8 discrete speedup categories** (a bucketed, direction-aware forecast). This is a deliberate design choice: relative/ordinal prediction is easier to calibrate and is exactly what a search loop needs (keep or discard this mutation).
- **Selective deferral / abstention.** Selectivity is implemented as **surrogate-filtered search**: "only candidates the surrogate predicts will be faster are run on a real GPU." Candidates the model predicts to be slower (or that fall in low-confidence buckets) are pruned without ever touching hardware — the abstention is a confidence-gated *defer-to-measurement* rule wired directly into the search's expansion step (a PUCT / predictor-guided tree search).
- **How RL improves calibration.** The surrogate (base model **GPT-OSS-20B**, trained with **LoRA**) is fine-tuned with **GRPO** under three reward configurations: (1) correctness only, (2) correctness **+ Brier score**, (3) correctness **+ CRPS**. The Brier and CRPS terms are proper scoring rules over the 8-class speedup distribution, so RL directly rewards *calibrated* probability estimates rather than just top-1 accuracy — the mechanism that makes the abstention threshold trustworthy.
- **Evaluation metrics.** Classification correctness on held-out pairs; **Brier score and CRPS** as calibration metrics; and the downstream systems metrics — number of candidates evaluated per fixed GPU budget, and the speedup of the best discovered kernel.
- **Baselines beaten / headline result.** Against search that measures every candidate on the GPU, surrogate-filtered search evaluates **several times more candidates within an equivalent GPU budget** and **discovers faster kernels** than the measure-everything baseline.
- **Released artifacts (reusable for FF4).** 424 held-out kernel pairs for evaluation; a training pool of parent-child pairs; raw PUCT search event logs; and LoRA adapters for GPT-OSS-20B under each of the three rewards. Runbook scripts reproduce results and resume from crashes.
- **Limitations (partly inferred — not confirmed from full text).** Predictions are relative/ordinal, not absolute runtimes; the surrogate is trained and tested within the KernelBench + GPU Mode distribution, so out-of-distribution kernels are untested; a single 20B base model; and the paper does not (in what was accessible) report false-negative cost — i.e., fast kernels wrongly pruned by abstention and thus never measured.

### Adjacent works

**KernelBench: Can LLMs Write Efficient GPU Kernels? (arXiv:2502.10517, 2025 — Scaling Intelligence Lab, Stanford)**
The benchmark GPU Forecasters is built on: given a PyTorch reference (250–270 workloads across 4 difficulty levels, from single ops to full architectures), the model must emit a custom GPU kernel that is functionally correct *and* faster. Introduces `fast_p` — the fraction of generated kernels that are both correct and exceed a speedup threshold *p* over the torch baseline. Frontier models beat the baseline in <20% of cases out of the box.
- Learnings for FF4: `fast_p` is the template for a **joint correctness-AND-improvement metric** — our OOM/throughput WM should be scored the same way (right failure-state prediction *and* right relative ranking), not on runtime error alone.
- The correct-vs-baseline framing maps cleanly onto our "action = proposed configuration, observation = measured outcome" contract; the reference implementation is the parent, the proposal is the child.

**AlphaEvolve: A Gemini-powered coding agent for algorithmic discovery (Google DeepMind white paper, 2025)**
An evolutionary coding agent that maintains a population of programs, mutates them with an LLM, and scores each with **automated evaluators**, feeding the best forward. Applied to GPU kernels (a 32% kernel speedup in Gemini's own training stack), matrix-multiplication algorithms (first improvement over Strassen in 56 years), and datacenter scheduling.
- Learnings for FF4: This is the canonical **measure-everything evolutionary loop** GPU Forecasters accelerates — it shows the ceiling of what a search finds when every candidate is truly evaluated, and thus the value of a surrogate is to reach that ceiling on a smaller compute budget.
- The "LLM proposes, evaluator verifies" split is exactly the seam where a pre-run WM estimator inserts: use the WM to rank/prune the population *before* spending real evaluations.

**Ithemal: Accurate, Portable and Fast Basic Block Throughput Estimation (arXiv:1808.07412, ICML 2019 — Mendis, Renda, Amarasinghe, Carbin)**
A hierarchical LSTM that predicts steady-state clock cycles (throughput) of a basic block from its opcodes/operands, with less than half the error of hand-built analytical models (LLVM `llvm-mca`, Intel IACA), and portable across microarchitectures by retraining on data.
- Learnings for FF4: The original argument that **learned models beat analytical/first-principles cost models** for hardware performance — directly supports our thesis that a WM trained on real traces can outdo hand-derived estimators.
- Portability-by-retraining is a precedent for our multi-hardware ambitions (vLLM throughput across GPU types): same architecture, new trace corpus per target.

**TLP + TenSet: learned cost models for tensor programs (TLP arXiv:2211.03578, ASPLOS 2023; TenSet, NeurIPS D&B 2021, https://github.com/tlc-pack/tenset)**
TLP treats tensor-program tuning as a language task — it feeds schedule primitives (as token sequences) to an LSTM to predict latency, avoiding hand-engineered features, with a multi-task extension for cross-hardware transfer. TenSet is the 52M-record performance dataset (6 hardware platforms, 120 workloads) that lets a pre-trained cost model cut tensor-compiler search time by up to 10×.
- Learnings for FF4: TenSet is the proof that **a large offline corpus of (program, measured latency) pairs is the key asset** — mirrors our OTel-trace strategy; consider a similar pre-train-then-specialize split.
- TLP's "schedule primitives as text" is a lighter-weight config encoding than full source, worth testing for our structured-tool-call actions (encode the *config*, not the whole program).

**SURGE: LLMs as General-Purpose Surrogate Code Executors (arXiv:2502.11167, EMNLP 2025)**
Benchmarks whether LLMs can predict execution outcomes across 1160 problems spanning 8 categories (multi-language, competition, repo-level, scientific computing, complexity-heavy, buggy code, environment-dependent, proof checking), evaluated over 21 open and proprietary models, studying scaling laws and data efficiency.
- Learnings for FF4: Provides **calibrated priors on where LLM surrogates are reliable vs. not** across code types — use its category breakdown to pick which of our v1 domains the WM can be trusted on first.
- Its scaling-law/data-efficiency analysis is a methodology template for our own "fidelity vs. #training-traces" curves.

**LASER: Language Model Regression for Semi-Structured Workflow Resource and Runtime Estimation (arXiv:2512.19701, 2025)**
Fine-tuned LLMs predict runtime and resource use of cloud/workflow jobs by serializing shell commands, tool params, dependency graphs, and metadata as text — no feature engineering. Uses scientific-notation encoding for wide-range numeric targets and constrained decoding for valid outputs; on GHARuntime (580k+ GitHub Actions runs) and chip-design workloads it beats human experts and SOTA tabular ML.
- Learnings for FF4: The closest neighbor to our estimator framing — **directly serializes structured configs to text and regresses a physical measurement**, exactly our action→observation setup for OOM/throughput.
- **Scientific-notation target encoding** and **constrained decoding** are concrete, adoptable tricks for emitting wide-range numbers (memory bytes, tokens/s) and enforcing valid failure-state outputs.

**Counting Without Running / gpuFLOPBench (arXiv:2512.04355, 2025 — Bolet et al.)**
Tests whether LLMs can predict single/double-precision FLOP counts for 577 CUDA kernels without executing them. Models nail simple kernels but make multi-order-of-magnitude errors when FLOPs are implicit (division, intrinsics, common subexpressions), and cannot internalize hardware microcode effects.
- Learnings for FF4: A sharp **failure-mode catalog** — the WM will be systematically wrong exactly where cost is hidden/implicit, which argues for the abstention mechanism and for feeding measured traces rather than expecting the model to reason it out.
- Motivates a "hard-case" slice in our eval so aggregate accuracy doesn't mask order-of-magnitude blowups.

**EvoEngineer: Automated CUDA Kernel Code Evolution with LLMs (arXiv:2510.03760, 2025)**
An LLM-driven evolutionary framework for CUDA kernels that manages mutation/crossover of kernel source under a correctness-and-speed objective, representative of the current wave of agentic kernel-search systems (alongside STARK, KForge).
- Learnings for FF4: Another instance of the expensive measure-every-candidate loop that a selective surrogate would accelerate — a candidate integration target/baseline for showing WM-in-the-loop savings.
- Its mutation operators define the realistic **distribution of "child" configs** our WM must generalize over.

### Takeaways for FF4

1. **Predict relative, bucketed outcomes — not absolute numbers — for the ranking/gating job.** GPU Forecasters' 8-class relative-speedup target calibrates far better than absolute-runtime regression and is what a search loop actually consumes. Use absolute regression (LASER-style) only where the downstream decision needs a magnitude (will this OOM at 80GB?).
2. **Make abstention a first-class output and gate real evaluation on it.** The core FF4 win is "surrogate-filtered search": run the expensive process only on candidates the WM endorses. Design the WM to emit a confidence/defer signal, and tune its threshold against the cost of a false prune (a fast config wrongly skipped) — a cost GPU Forecasters itself does not report, so we should measure it.
3. **Train calibration with proper scoring rules via RL.** Their GRPO + Brier/CRPS rewards are directly reusable: calibrated probabilities, not top-1 accuracy, are what make the abstention gate safe. Adopt Brier/CRPS as both training reward and reported metric.
4. **Encode the action as an edit (parent→child) plus the structured config, in text.** Presenting the mutation relative to a known-good parent is a strong prior; TLP shows encoding just the config/schedule (not the whole program) can suffice. Test both encodings for our structured-tool-call actions.
5. **Score with a joint correct-AND-better metric (`fast_p`) and carve out a hard-case slice.** Correctness of the failure-state prediction and correctness of the relative ranking must both count; add a gpuFLOPBench-style slice of implicit-cost cases so aggregate numbers don't hide order-of-magnitude misses.
6. **Reusable assets exist — start from them.** GPU Forecasters ships 424 eval pairs, a parent-child training pool, PUCT search logs, and GPT-OSS-20B LoRA adapters; TenSet offers 52M tensor-program latency records; KernelBench and EvoEngineer provide the search harnesses. These let FF4's kernel/ML-memory domain bootstrap without capturing traces from scratch.
## LLMs as world models & environment simulators

**Reasoning with Language Model is Planning with World Model (RAP) (EMNLP 2023, [aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.507](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.507/))**
Hao et al. repurpose a single LLM as *both* a policy and a world model: the world model predicts the next state given a state–action pair, and MCTS uses those predicted rollouts to plan over reasoning steps for plan generation, math, and logic. This is the foundational "the LLM already contains a world model, you just have to query it as one" argument, and it establishes the action→state-transition framing that later work inherits. Code is available (`llm-reasoners`).
Learnings for FF4:
- The core primitive we need — `predict(state, action) → next_state` — is exactly RAP's world-model call; FF4's version just returns measured numbers/failure states instead of a text state.
- Planning quality is bottlenecked by transition fidelity: RAP works on short symbolic horizons but its self-consistency crutch hints that single-shot transition prediction is noisy. For a pre-run *estimator* we don't need multi-step rollout, which sidesteps RAP's compounding-error weakness.

**Language Models Meet World Models: Embodied Experiences Enhance Language Models (NeurIPS 2023, [arxiv.org/abs/2305.10626](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10626))**
Xiang et al. run the arrow the other way: they deploy an agent in a physical-world simulator (VirtualHome), harvest goal-directed and random-exploration trajectories, then fine-tune the LLM on that experience (with EWC + LoRA to avoid catastrophic forgetting) to improve planning, object tracking, and object permanence. The finding that matters is that grounding an LLM in *logged simulator interaction* measurably improves its world reasoning, and that you must protect general capability while doing it. Code released.
Learnings for FF4:
- This is the training-side justification for FF4: fine-tuning on real logged runs (our OTel traces) is a known lever for turning a generic LLM into a competent world model of a specific process.
- EWC/LoRA + "keep general ability" is a concrete recipe — worth mirroring so the OOM/throughput specialist doesn't lose the language priors that let it generalize across configs it never saw.

**Can Language Models Serve as Text-Based World Simulators? (ByteSized32-SP) (ACL 2024 short, [aclanthology.org/2024.acl-short.1](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-short.1/))**
Wang et al. build ByteSized32-State-Prediction, which asks an LLM to predict the *full JSON game state* after an action, decomposed into action-driven transitions, environment-driven ("what changes on its own") transitions, and reward. GPT-4 tops out around 59.9% full-state accuracy versus ~80% for humans; crucially it hits ~77% on action-driven transitions but only ~49.7% on environment-driven ones, and errors cluster exactly where arithmetic, physics, or commonsense computation is required (temperature, filling volumes). This is the single best-quantified statement of where pure text simulation fails.
Learnings for FF4:
- The failure mode is *our* failure mode: environment-driven, arithmetic-heavy state changes are the hardest, and FF4's targets (memory bytes, throughput, timing closure) are precisely arithmetic/physics quantities. Naive prompting will not clear ~60% here.
- Their decomposition (action-driven vs. environment-driven vs. reward) is a ready-made evaluation harness — score FF4 separately on "does the config parse/apply" vs. "does the measured number come out right" vs. "did it OOM/fail."
- Predicting *state deltas* rather than the whole state was measurably easier for them; FF4 should predict the outcome vector + failure flag, not reconstruct the whole machine state.

**Web Agents with World Models (WMA) (ICLR 2025, [arxiv.org/abs/2410.13232](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13232))**
Chae et al. train an LLM world model for web navigation and use its predicted next-observation to let the agent avoid irreversible/harmful actions (e.g., buying a non-refundable ticket) without live interaction. Their key trick is **transition-focused observation abstraction**: instead of predicting the full next HTML (long, repetitive), the model emits a free-form natural-language description of only the salient *differences* between steps. This beats reactive baselines on WebArena/Mind2Web and is cheaper than tree-search agents.
Learnings for FF4:
- "Predict the diff, not the world" generalizes directly — FF4 should emit the *changed measured quantities and any new failure state*, not re-describe the whole run environment.
- The explicit motivation is avoiding costly/irreversible actions via simulated look-ahead — this is the same value proposition as FF4's expensive-process estimator, validated on a real benchmark.

**Is Your LLM Secretly a World Model of the Internet? (WebDreamer) (ICLR 2025, [arxiv.org/abs/2411.06559](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.06559))**
Gu, Zheng et al. use an off-the-shelf LLM in an MPC loop: for each candidate action it imagines the resulting page state in natural language ("what would happen if I click this?"), scores the imagined trajectories, and picks the best — improving over reactive baselines on VisualWebArena and Mind2Web-live with no environment access at plan time. It's the cleanest demonstration that a *frozen* LLM already encodes enough dynamics to plan, trading fidelity for zero-cost simulation and safety.
Learnings for FF4:
- Confirms a zero-shot baseline is worth measuring before we invest in trace-fine-tuning — how far does a frozen model get on OOM prediction from config alone?
- MPC-style "simulate N candidate configs, score, rank" is exactly the FF4 launch use case (propose configuration → estimated outcome → choose), so their scoring/selection loop is a reusable pattern.

**τ-bench: A Benchmark for Tool-Agent-User Interaction (NeurIPS 2024, [arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045))**
Yao et al. (Sierra) build environments where an **LLM simulates the human user** while the agent calls domain APIs under policy constraints; evaluation compares final database state to a goal state and introduces pass^k to measure reliability across trials. Even GPT-4o passes <50% and is highly inconsistent (pass^8 <25% in retail). This is the canonical "LLM as environment/user simulator" harness and its state-based, execution-grounded scoring is directly relevant.
Learnings for FF4:
- The evaluation philosophy — *compare final measured state to ground-truth state*, not surface text — is exactly how FF4 should be scored against logged run outcomes.
- pass^k exposes that LLM simulators are *inconsistent*, not just inaccurate; FF4 needs variance/calibration metrics, not just point accuracy, especially for safety-critical OOM calls.
- Later follow-ups (e.g. "Sim2Real gap in user simulation") flag that simulated actors drift from real ones — a caution that our trace-grounding is what keeps the simulator honest.

**AgentGym: Evolving LLM Agents across Diverse Environments (ACL 2025, [github.com/WooooDyy/AgentGym](https://github.com/WooooDyy/AgentGym))**
Xi et al. provide 14 environments (WebShop, WebArena, BabyAI, SciWorld, TextCraft, embodied tasks) behind a uniform HTTP API for reset/observe/act, decoupling environment simulation from the agent and enabling cross-environment training and later multi-turn RL (AgentGym-RL). It's the reference architecture for exposing many heterogeneous environments through one standardized action/observation interface.
Learnings for FF4:
- The clean `reset / observe / act` service boundary is the right shape for FF4's three v1 domains (OOM, vLLM throughput, FPGA P&R) — one serving interface, swappable domain world models behind it.
- Their explicit caveat that *LLM-simulated* environments hallucinate and lack persistent state (hence they prefer sandboxed executable envs) is the argument for FF4 grounding predictions in real measured traces rather than free generation.

**Simia: Simulating Environments with Reasoning Models for Agent Training (2025, [arxiv.org/abs/2511.01824](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.01824))**
This work has an LLM act as user, agent, *and* environment to amplify a small set of seed trajectories into diverse synthetic training data (Simia-SFT) and to supply simulated feedback for RL without any real environment (Simia-RL), reporting solid gains fine-tuning Qwen models. It represents the aggressive end of the spectrum: ~90k transitions synthesized from a handful of seeds, no live environment access.
Learnings for FF4:
- Seed-amplification is attractive because our real runs are *expensive* — a few hundred logged OOM/throughput traces could seed many plausible configs, if fidelity holds.
- But this is the cautionary contrast to FF4's thesis: fully synthetic transitions have no ground-truth anchor, so they're fine for teaching *format/coverage* but dangerous for the *numeric* accuracy FF4 sells. Use seed-amplification for breadth, real traces for calibration.

**DreamGym: Scaling Agent Learning via Experience Synthesis (2025, [arxiv.org/abs/2511.03773](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.03773))**
Meta's DreamGym distills environment dynamics into a *reasoning-based experience model* that produces state transitions and reward via step-by-step reasoning; a replay buffer **initialized with offline real-world data and continuously enriched with fresh interactions** plus a curriculum task generator make RL cheap. It beats baselines by >30% on non-RL-ready WebArena, matches GRPO/PPO with only synthetic rollouts, and gives extra gains when the synthetically-trained policy transfers to real-environment RL. This is the closest published cousin to FF4's design.
Learnings for FF4:
- The offline-real-data-seeded, reasoning-based transition model *is* FF4's architecture; their >30% result is direct external validation that a trace-grounded LLM world model works as a training/estimation substrate.
- The "reasoning-based" transition generation (make the model explain the mechanism before emitting the outcome) is worth adopting for numeric targets — chain-of-thought on the physics of memory/throughput before predicting the number.
- Their sim→real transfer gain supports positioning FF4 as a cheap *pre-run* filter that still defers to real measurement for the final decision.

**Genie 2: A Large-Scale Foundation World Model (DeepMind, 2024, [deepmind.google/blog/genie-2](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-2-a-large-scale-foundation-world-model/)) — contrast entry**
Genie 2 learns a latent-action world model from raw video, generating physically consistent, playable 3D environments frame-by-frame with persistent memory. It's the pixel/latent-space analogue of what we do in text: high visual fidelity and emergent dynamics, but no symbolic access to the exact quantities (bytes, tokens/s, timing slack) a decision-maker actually needs, and it's trained on passive observation rather than structured logged outcomes.
Learnings for FF4:
- Frames the design choice: latent/pixel world models win on rich perceptual dynamics; **text/structured world models win when the decision variable is a number or a discrete failure state** — which is FF4's entire domain.
- Their persistent-memory / long-horizon consistency is the known hard problem; FF4 sidesteps it by being a single-step estimator rather than a rollout simulator, which is a deliberate scoping advantage.

**Agentic Environment Engineering: A Survey of Environment Modeling, Synthesis, Evaluation, and Application (2026, [arxiv.org/abs/2606.12191](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12191)) — framing entry**
A recent survey organizing the fast-moving 2025–2026 literature on how environments for LLM agents are modeled, synthesized (LLM-simulated vs. sandboxed-executable vs. trace-harvested), evaluated for fidelity, and deployed. It usefully catalogs the recurring tension between cheap-but-hallucinatory LLM simulation and expensive-but-faithful executable/logged environments, and the retrieval-augmented "ground each prediction in a real tutorial/trajectory" pattern reported to add double-digit procedural-alignment gains.
Learnings for FF4:
- Situates FF4 squarely in the "trace-harvested + retrieval-augmented" quadrant — the quadrant the survey associates with the best fidelity, justifying the OTel-trace grounding over free generation.
- Retrieval-over-real-trajectories (cf. our DreamGym-style retrieval and the WMH retrieval work) is a repeatedly-validated fidelity lever; worth making a first-class input to the FF4 estimator, not an afterthought.

### Takeaways for FF4

1. **Where text simulation is known to break is exactly FF4's target, so this is the whole risk.** ByteSized32-SP pins the failure at environment-driven, arithmetic/physics-driven state changes (GPT-4 ~50% vs. ~77% on simple action effects), and Genie-class work shows numeric quantities are precisely what latent simulators can't expose. FF4 predicts bytes, tokens/s, and timing closure — the hard cases — so the entire value hinges on beating the ~60% frozen-model ceiling, which is what grounding is for.

2. **Grounding in logged traces is the demonstrated lever, and retrieval + fine-tuning are the two proven forms.** "Language Models Meet World Models" (fine-tune on logged simulator experience) and DreamGym (offline-real-data-seeded replay + reasoning transitions, >30% gains) both show that real interaction data turns a generic LLM into a competent process-specific world model. Retrieval-augmented grounding adds further double-digit gains in the surveys. FF4's OTel corpus should feed *both* fine-tuning and retrieval.

3. **Predict the outcome delta and the failure flag, not the whole world.** WMA's transition-focused abstraction and ByteSized32's state-diff mode both show that emitting only the salient changes is easier and more accurate than reconstructing full state. FF4 should output the measured-outcome vector plus discrete failure states (OOM, timing-fail) — nothing more.

4. **Score against measured ground-truth state, and measure consistency, not just accuracy.** τ-bench scores by comparing final DB state to a goal state, and pass^k reveals LLM simulators are dangerously inconsistent. FF4 needs calibration and variance metrics against logged outcomes — a single-point accuracy number is insufficient for safety-critical OOM predictions.

5. **Scope FF4 as a single-step pre-run estimator, not a multi-step rollout — that dodges the field's worst failure mode.** RAP, WebDreamer, and Genie all struggle with compounding error and long-horizon consistency. FF4's action→measured-outcome estimator is inherently single-step, turning a known weakness into a non-issue, and the MPC-style "propose N configs, estimate, rank" loop (WebDreamer, DreamGym transfer) is the validated deployment pattern.

6. **Fully-synthetic simulation is fine for coverage but cannot be trusted for the numbers — keep real measurement in the loop.** Simia synthesizes 90k transitions from seeds with no ground truth, and AgentGym/τ-bench follow-ups warn of hallucination and sim2real drift. Position FF4 as a cheap filter that narrows the search and still defers the final expensive run to real hardware, so its errors are recoverable rather than authoritative.
## Language as compression of reality (internal background — not launch framing)

The premise of FF4's world model — that an LLM already carries compressed priors about how the computational and physical world behaves, and that a few hundred real measurement traces specialize that prior into a usable model of *your* hardware — rests on a well-developed body of theory and evidence linking compression, prediction, and world-modeling. This section surveys that literature and marks precisely where it supports our framing and where it does not.

**Solomonoff Induction, Kolmogorov complexity, and Hutter's AIXI / Hutter Prize ([Hutter, *Universal AI*](https://www.hutter1.net/publ/suaigentle.pdf); [Hutter Prize](https://aivips.org/marcus-hutter/), 2006–present)**
The formal root of "compression is intelligence." Solomonoff induction defines the ideal predictor as a universal prior over all computable programs; its optimal codelength for any data string is that string's Kolmogorov complexity (the length of the shortest program that outputs it). Hutter's AIXI extends this from prediction to action, defining an optimal agent that maximizes expected reward in any computable environment. The Hutter Prize operationalizes the thesis directly by rewarding better lossless compression of Wikipedia, on the argument that compressing text well *requires* modeling the regularities that generated it.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Establishes the theoretical warrant that a good predictor of measurement traces is implicitly a model of the process generating them. Grounds our claim that next-token prediction over run traces is not mere pattern-matching but (approximate) inference of the underlying system dynamics. Caution: Kolmogorov complexity is *uncomputable* — LLMs are bounded, lossy approximations, so "optimal" is aspirational, not achieved.

**Language Modeling Is Compression ([Delétang et al., DeepMind, ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10668))**
Makes the prediction↔compression equivalence empirical rather than theoretical: any probabilistic language model can be turned into a lossless compressor via arithmetic coding, with expected codelength equal to the model's cross-entropy. Strikingly, Chinchilla 70B — trained only on text — compresses ImageNet image patches to 43.4% and LibriSpeech audio to 16.4% of raw size, beating the domain-specific PNG (58.5%) and FLAC (30.3%). The compression lens also yields fresh insight into scaling laws, tokenization, and in-context learning.
- **Learnings for FF4:** The single most important citation for our thesis. It shows a text-trained model's priors *transfer across modalities* — the model has learned general structure, not just linguistic surface. This is the mechanistic basis for "language is a compression of reality that transfers to your telemetry." It also reframes our fine-tuning: adapting the model on traces is lowering its cross-entropy on your hardware's data, i.e. making it a better compressor of your world.

**Compression Represents Intelligence Linearly ([Huang, Zhang, Shan & He, COLM 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09937))**
The first systematic empirical evidence that an LLM's compression ability tracks its measured capability. Across many models spanning sizes, tokenizers, context lengths, and pretraining mixes, average benchmark score correlates *almost linearly* (Pearson ≈ 0.95) with bits-per-character on held-out corpora, making compression efficiency a cheap, unsupervised, label-free proxy for intelligence.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Licenses using compression/perplexity on held-out traces as a leading indicator of world-model quality — before we've paid to run the expensive process we're trying to predict. Suggests a monitoring metric for FF4: does fine-tuning lower BPC on held-out hardware traces? Caveat below (the linearity is contested).

**Is Compression Really Linear with Code Intelligence? ([Xuyang et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11441))**
The essential counterweight to Huang et al. Evaluating across multiple programming languages and tasks with a fairer "Format Annealing" protocol, the authors find the true relationship is *logarithmic*, not linear — earlier linear fits were "the logarithmic curve's tail under specific, limited conditions." Compression still tracks capability, but with diminishing returns.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Directly disciplines our claims. We should say compression *predicts* capability monotonically, not that halving BPC halves error. Expect diminishing returns: the first traces buy the most world-model fidelity, later ones less — which is actually the economic argument *for* our "few hundred traces" pitch, and consistent with our own trace-scaling-law finding that RAG fidelity saturates fast.

**An Observation on Generalization ([Sutskever, Simons Institute, 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AKMuA_TVz3A))**
Sutskever's compression account of *unsupervised* learning: a compressor that jointly compresses an unlabeled dataset X together with a task dataset Y will discover and exploit the structure shared between them — this shared structure is exactly what makes pretraining transfer to downstream tasks. He frames the theoretical ceiling as a "Kolmogorov compressor" and argues large models are practical approximations of it.
- **Learnings for FF4:** The clean articulation of *why grounding works*: jointly compressing the model's broad pretraining world-knowledge with our narrow hardware traces surfaces the shared physical/computational structure. Our few hundred traces don't teach the model physics from scratch — they let it index the physics it already compressed onto *our* machines.

**Principles of Parsimony and Self-Consistency / White-Box Transformers via Sparse Rate Reduction ([Ma, Tsao & Shum, FITEE 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.04630); [Yu et al., JMLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.13110))**
Yi Ma and collaborators argue intelligence emerges from two principles: parsimony (compress data to a low-dimensional, sparse structure via "rate reduction") and self-consistency. In the white-box "CRATE" architecture, each transformer block is *derived* as an optimization step on a compression objective — self-attention approximately performs gradient descent on the coding rate, and the MLP sparsifies — showing transformer computation can be read as literal compression, not just correlated with it.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Provides an architectural, mechanistic reading of "the model is compressing," strengthening the framing beyond information-theoretic analogy. Useful for a defensible technical narrative: the world model isn't a black box we hope compresses reality — compression is a candidate account of what its layers *do*. Use cautiously: CRATE is a research architecture; production LLMs are only *approximately* interpretable this way.

**Emergent World Representations: Othello-GPT ([Li, Hopkins, Bau, Viégas, Pfister & Wattenberg, ICLR 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13382))**
A GPT trained only on sequences of legal Othello moves — never shown the board or rules — develops an internal representation of board state that can be *causally* intervened on to steer its predictions. Follow-up work ([Nanda](https://www.neelnanda.io/mechanistic-interpretability/othello)) showed this world model is linearly decodable. Landmark evidence that next-token prediction over surface tokens induces a genuine model of the underlying generative process.
- **Learnings for FF4:** The best existence proof that "predict the next token" yields an internal world model, not memorized statistics — and that it is *probeable and steerable*. Analogy for FF4: trained on traces of runs, the model can form an internal representation of hardware state (memory pressure, contention, routing) that drives its outcome estimates. Motivates probing our WM's internals to validate it learned mechanism, not surface correlation.

**From Word Models to World Models ([Wong, Grand, Lew, Goodman, Mansinghka, Andreas & Tenenbaum, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12672))**
Proposes "rational meaning construction": LLMs translate natural language into a probabilistic language of thought (executable symbolic programs) over which Bayesian inference — not the LLM — does the coherent reasoning, spanning probabilistic, relational, physical, and social domains. A deliberate statement that LLMs are powerful *interfaces to* world models rather than complete world models themselves.
- **Learnings for FF4:** A useful sobering counterpoint and a design option. It argues the strongest systems pair LLM priors with structured inference. For FF4 this suggests hybrid framings (LLM prior + calibrated estimator over measured traces) may be more defensible than "the LLM *is* the world model," and warns against over-claiming pure-LLM reasoning fidelity for quantitative outcomes.

**Physical-world knowledge probes: Linear Spatial World Models ([Tehenan, Moya, Long & Lin, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02996)); intuitive-physics evidence — V-JEPA ([Garrido et al., Meta, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11831)) and its limits — Pixels to Principles ([2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16572))**
The most recent, and most double-edged, evidence. Tehenan et al. show LLMs linearly encode spatial relations ("left" = −"right", orthogonal independent axes) and use them causally — physical structure genuinely lives in the weights. But the vision-language physics benchmarks are sobering: V-JEPA, a *video*-pretrained predictive model, reaches 98% on the IntPhys violation-of-expectation benchmark while text- and pixel-prediction models sit near chance, and Pixels to Principles finds multimodal LLMs' physical-plausibility judgments degrade precisely at the vision-language interface.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Cuts both ways and must be cited honestly. LLMs *do* carry real, causally-used physical priors (supports us), but understanding learned from the right modality/grounding vastly outperforms understanding inferred second-hand from text (V-JEPA ≫ text models on physics). The lesson for FF4: our real OTel measurement traces are the "video" — the grounded modality that turns weak textual physics priors into a quantitatively reliable world model of specific hardware.

### Takeaways for FF4

1. **What the literature *does* license us to claim.** There is a rigorous, empirically-supported chain — Solomonoff/Hutter → Delétang → Huang → Sutskever — that (a) prediction and compression are formally equivalent, (b) a text-trained model's compressed priors demonstrably transfer to non-text data (Chinchilla compressing images/audio), and (c) better compression tracks greater capability. This fully supports the framing "an LLM is a lossy compression of reality that already encodes broad priors about computational and physical systems." Othello-GPT and the spatial-world-model probes let us add that these priors are genuine internal world models, not surface statistics.

2. **What it does *not* license — over-claims to avoid.** (i) Do *not* say the model achieves optimal or Kolmogorov compression; it is a bounded, lossy approximation. (ii) Do *not* claim a *linear* dose-response between compression and accuracy — the code-intelligence result shows it is logarithmic, with diminishing returns. (iii) Do *not* claim text pretraining alone yields quantitatively reliable physics — the V-JEPA vs. text-model gap shows grounding in the right modality is what makes physical understanding accurate. (iv) Do *not* conflate "encodes physical structure" (true) with "reasons correctly about novel physical quantities without grounding" (contested; Pixels-to-Principles and From-Word-Models both caution here).

3. **Grounding is the load-bearing move, and the literature says exactly why.** Sutskever's joint-compression argument and the V-JEPA modality result converge on the same point: the model's broad priors become a *usable, specific* world model only when jointly compressed with real data from the target domain. Our few hundred OTel traces are that grounding — the "video," not the "text." Phrase it as: *the prior supplies the physics, the traces supply your hardware.*

4. **"Few hundred traces" is defensible precisely because of diminishing returns.** The logarithmic compression↔capability curve (Xuyang et al.) and our own saturating trace-scaling law are the *same* phenomenon and they are a feature, not a bug: the first traces carry the most information about your system, so a small corpus captures most of the achievable fidelity. Lead with this rather than hiding it.

5. **Frame FF4 as a calibrated estimator, not an oracle.** From-Word-Models-to-World-Models argues the strongest systems pair LLM priors with structured inference over grounded evidence. For the launch narrative, "prior + measured traces → calibrated outcome estimate with uncertainty" is both more accurate and more credible than "the LLM predicts your OOM." Report intervals; the pre-run estimator's value is de-risking expensive runs, not replacing them.

6. **Defensible one-line launch narrative.** "Language models compress the regularities of the world into reusable priors (Delétang 2024; and the priors are real internal world models — Li 2023, Tehenan 2025). Compression ability tracks capability (Huang 2024), but grounding in the target modality is what makes it *quantitatively* reliable (V-JEPA, 2025). FF4 grounds that prior with a few hundred real OpenTelemetry traces of your hardware, specializing a general world model into a calibrated pre-run estimator for expensive processes." Every clause is citation-backed and none of them over-reaches.
## LLMs as numeric regressors & in-context function learners

This body of work asks whether language models can map structured inputs to numeric outputs — either fine-tuned end-to-end or purely in-context from retrieved (x, y) pairs. It is the closest published analogue to FF4's core move: given a config as text, predict a measured number (and, for us, a failure flag). Below, entries are ordered roughly by relevance to a retrieval-augmented pre-run estimator, with the two most directly load-bearing papers (OmniPred, "From Words to Numbers") first.

**OmniPred: Language Models as Universal Regressors (TMLR 2024, [arxiv.org/abs/2402.14547](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14547))**
Trains a 200M-parameter T5 encoder-decoder *from scratch* to regress the outcome `y` of a trial directly from its textual `(x, metadata)` representation, using ~Google Vizier's blackbox-optimization history across thousands of studies. Numbers are emitted digit-by-digit in a sign/mantissa/exponent scheme (e.g. `1.23` → `<+><1><2><3><E-2>`), and the model is scored by per-study normalized MAE (absolute error divided by the study's y-range, clipped at 1.0). The headline result: a single multi-task model reaches ~0.15 normalized error on AutoML data versus ~0.42 for a Gaussian Process, ~0.32 for Random Forest, and ~0.98 for a single-task MLP — and after pretraining, finetuning on 1000 unseen studies beats from-scratch baselines (~0.21 vs ~0.28). Ablating parameter names/metadata degrades error from 0.19 to 0.44, showing that textual signal drives cross-task transfer.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - This is the strongest existence proof that an LM regresses measured numeric outcomes from structured configs across heterogeneous tasks — exactly FF4's setting. The multi-task-beats-single-task result is the argument for training one WM over all sweep domains rather than per-domain surrogates.
  - The digit-by-digit sign/exponent tokenization is a concrete, validated default for emitting throughput/memory numbers; they explicitly found it best in low-data regimes.
  - The metadata ablation says: put the config's semantic field names and task description in the prompt verbatim — the transfer benefit comes from text the LM can ground, not from raw numbers alone. Code: `google-research/optformer` (omnipred).

**From Words to Numbers: Your LLM Is Secretly A Capable Regressor When Given In-Context Examples (COLM 2024, [arxiv.org/abs/2404.07544](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07544))**
Shows that *frozen* pretrained LLMs (GPT-4, Claude 3, Llama2) do linear and non-linear regression purely from in-context `(x, y)` pairs with no weight updates, rivaling or beating Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, KNN, and SVM — e.g. Claude 3 outperforms most supervised baselines on Friedman #2. They frame it through online-learning theory and show cumulative regret grows *sub-linearly* in the number of in-context examples, i.e. each retrieved example helps with diminishing but consistent returns.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - This is the direct precedent for FF4's retrieval-augmented *in-context* regression — no finetuning required, which validates the "retrieved transitions in the prompt, then predict" design.
  - Sub-linear regret is the theoretical hook for choosing k: more retrieved neighbors monotonically help but with diminishing returns, so tune k empirically rather than assuming "more is always better."
  - Frontier chat models (Claude 3 / GPT-4) were the strong performers, supporting FF4's use of a capable base model rather than a tiny specialized head. Code: [github.com/robertvacareanu/llm4regression](https://github.com/robertvacareanu/llm4regression).

**LLM Processes: Numerical Predictive Distributions Conditioned on Natural Language (NeurIPS 2024, [arxiv.org/abs/2405.12856](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12856))**
Turns an LLM into a stochastic process that outputs a joint *predictive distribution* over query points, conditioned both on numeric context pairs and on a natural-language prior. Extracts calibrated densities by sampling/decoding numeric completions, and shows the result is competitive with Gaussian Processes, LLMTime, and Optuna on forecasting, multi-dim regression, and black-box optimization, while handling missing data and multimodal predictions.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - Gives a recipe for *uncertainty* on FF4's numeric predictions: sample the number token stream to get a full predictive distribution, not just a point estimate — useful for flagging low-confidence throughput/memory predictions before committing a run.
  - Demonstrates in-context leveraging of *related* datasets automatically, which is exactly what retrieval from sweep history provides.
  - The natural-language-prior channel suggests FF4 can inject known constraints ("batch size 512 usually OOMs on 40GB") as text. Code: [github.com/requeima/llm_processes](https://github.com/requeima/llm_processes).

**Decoding-based Regression (2025, [arxiv.org/abs/2501.19383](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19383))**
Song & Bahri (Google DeepMind) study causal decoding of numeric strings as a general regression head on top of any feature representation. Trained with ordinary next-token cross-entropy, digit-decoding heads (e.g. `<1><.><2><3>`) match standard pointwise (MSE) heads on tabular regression while additionally capturing smooth conditional densities — outperforming Gaussian-mixture and histogram density heads. They give a theoretical risk-minimization analysis of the tokenization for both normalized and unnormalized targets.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - Justifies representing the predicted measurement as decoded digit tokens (rather than a regression head) without accuracy loss — the WM stays a plain LM, keeping the OOM-flag and numeric prediction in one decode.
  - The density-estimation angle is a second route to calibrated uncertainty for FF4 outputs.
  - Normalization of the target before tokenization matters for the risk bound — normalize measurements per-metric/per-domain. A 2025 follow-up adds RL over token-level supervision ([arxiv.org/abs/2512.06533](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06533)).

**Understanding LLM Embeddings for Regression (TMLR/2024, [arxiv.org/abs/2411.14708](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.14708))**
Tang et al. (DeepMind/Stanford) study using LLM *embeddings* of the stringified input as features for a downstream regressor. Finds embedding-based regression can beat traditional feature engineering especially in high dimensions, and that LLM embeddings over numeric inputs tend to preserve Lipschitz continuity over the feature space. Surprisingly, larger models and better language understanding do *not* reliably improve regression quality.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - Offers a cheaper alternative/complement to full decoding: embed the config and regress — worth benchmarking against FF4's in-context approach as an ablation.
  - The "bigger model ≠ better regression" finding is a caution against assuming FF4 needs the largest base model; capacity should be justified empirically.
  - Lipschitz preservation implies nearby configs get nearby embeddings, which is the property that makes retrieval-by-similarity meaningful for FF4.

**What Can Transformers Learn In-Context? A Case Study of Simple Function Classes (NeurIPS 2022, [arxiv.org/abs/2208.01066](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.01066))**
Garg, Tsipras, Liang & Valiant show transformers trained from scratch can in-context learn linear functions matching the optimal least-squares estimator, and extend to sparse linear functions, two-layer nets, and decision trees — including under distribution shift between prompts and queries. This is the foundational evidence that in-context learning *is* function fitting.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - Theoretical grounding for why retrieved `(config → measurement)` pairs let the WM "fit" the local response surface at inference time.
  - The robustness-to-distribution-shift result is encouraging for FF4 extrapolating slightly beyond the exact configs seen in sweep history — but note this was for clean synthetic classes, so validate on real OOM boundaries. Code: [github.com/dtsip/in-context-learning](https://github.com/dtsip/in-context-learning).

**Large Language Models Are Zero-Shot Time Series Forecasters / LLMTime (NeurIPS 2023, [arxiv.org/abs/2310.07820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07820))**
Gruver et al. encode a numeric series as a string of digits and forecast via next-token sampling, matching or beating purpose-built forecasters zero-shot. Central lesson is that *tokenization is decisive*: they space out digits and control precision so that each digit is a token, and convert the discrete token distribution into a flexible continuous density; performance scales with base-model quality.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - The most practical tokenization reference — spacing digits and fixing precision so the tokenizer doesn't merge "512" into one opaque token is likely necessary for FF4's numeric fidelity.
  - Sampling completions to form a density transfers directly to FF4 uncertainty estimates. Code: [github.com/ngruver/llmtime](https://github.com/ngruver/llmtime).

**Chronos: Learning the Language of Time Series (2024, [arxiv.org/abs/2403.07815](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07815)) — contrast**
Ansari et al. (Amazon) tokenize series via scaling + *quantization into a fixed vocabulary* and pretrain T5-family models (20M–710M) with cross-entropy on a large real+synthetic corpus, achieving strong zero-shot forecasting across 42 datasets.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - The instructive *contrast* to digit-level schemes: Chronos bins values into a fixed vocabulary rather than decoding digits. FF4 should decide deliberately between quantization (bounded vocab, loses fine precision) and digit decoding (arbitrary precision, longer sequences); OmniPred/LLMTime argue for digits when precision matters, Chronos shows quantization scales well when a big pretraining corpus is available.
  - Reinforces that a purpose-pretrained small model on domain traces can be very competitive — relevant if FF4 ever moves from in-context to a trained WM.

**xVal: A Continuous Numerical Tokenization for Scientific Language Models (2023, [arxiv.org/abs/2310.02989](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02989))**
Encodes any real number with a *single* token whose embedding is scaled by the value, giving a continuous inductive bias and shorter sequences; outperforms digit/multi-digit schemes on OOD generalization and compute efficiency for scientific data.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - The counterpoint to digit-by-digit: if FF4 sequence lengths blow up with many retrieved numeric transitions, a continuous encoding trades some precision for large context savings. Requires model-side changes (custom embedding), so it is a heavier option than prompt-level formatting.
  - Highlights that default BPE number tokenization is a real failure mode worth measuring before choosing a scheme.

**LLAMBO: Large Language Models to Enhance Bayesian Optimization (ICLR 2024, [arxiv.org/abs/2402.03921](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03921))**
Liu et al. frame Bayesian optimization in natural language and use an LLM (no finetuning) for zero-shot warmstarting, surrogate modeling, and candidate sampling, conditioning on the history of evaluated configs. Strongest in the *early, data-sparse* regime; modular so components drop into existing BO loops.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - Directly relevant framing: LLAMBO's "surrogate model" component *is* an LLM predicting an objective value from a config given history — the same object as the FF4 WM, but used inside an optimizer loop. FF4 could serve as this surrogate.
  - The "shines when observations are sparse" finding suggests FF4's value is highest early in a sweep, before enough points exist to fit a classical GP. Code: [github.com/tennisonliu/LLAMBO](https://github.com/tennisonliu/LLAMBO).

**Can LLMs Beat Classical Hyperparameter Optimization Algorithms? (2026, [arxiv.org/abs/2603.24647](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24647))**
Ferreira, Hutter et al. benchmark LLM agents against CMA-ES/TPE for HPO under a fixed compute budget. Classical methods consistently win when the search space is fixed and *avoiding out-of-memory failures matters more than search diversity*; frontier models (Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro) narrow but do not close the gap, and LLMs specifically struggle to *track optimization state across trials*. They propose Centaur, a hybrid exposing CMA-ES's interpretable state to the LLM.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - The most sobering and most on-point entry: it names *OOM avoidance* as the exact axis where LLM-driven search underperforms — this is FF4's failure-flag target, so it is both the motivation (OOM prediction is hard and valuable) and a warning that the WM must be rigorously validated on the failure boundary, not just central-tendency error.
  - "LLMs struggle to track state across trials" argues for FF4's design of feeding retrieved history explicitly in the prompt rather than expecting the model to maintain state.
  - Hybrid framing (Centaur) suggests FF4 is best positioned as a *pre-run estimator feeding a classical optimizer*, not a replacement for it. Code: [github.com/ferreirafabio/autoresearch-automl](https://github.com/ferreirafabio/autoresearch-automl).

**Uncertainty Quantification and Confidence Calibration in LLMs: A Survey (KDD 2025, [arxiv.org/abs/2503.15850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15850))**
Surveys UQ methods for LLMs: logit/probability-based, sampling-consistency/entropy, verbalized confidence, internal-representation probing, and trained calibration heads. Recurring finding across the literature: RLHF-tuned models are systematically *overconfident*, emit verbalized confidences clustered in 80–100%, and reach ECE ≥ 0.30 on hard tasks.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - Do *not* trust a WM-verbalized "I'm 95% sure this OOMs" — that number is likely miscalibrated. Prefer sampling-based dispersion (à la LLM Processes / LLMTime) or a trained calibration head, and report Expected Calibration Error.
  - Especially important for the binary OOM flag, where an overconfident false-negative wastes a real run.

### Takeaways for FF4

1. **Number formatting is a first-class design decision, not a detail.** The consistent lesson from OmniPred, LLMTime, and xVal is that default BPE tokenization mangles numbers. Concrete default: emit and format measurements digit-by-digit with explicit sign and exponent (OmniPred's `<+><1><2><3><E-2>`) or space out digits at fixed precision (LLMTime). Normalize each metric to a stable range before tokenizing (Decoding-based Regression's risk analysis). Consider a single-token continuous scheme (xVal) only if retrieved-context length becomes the bottleneck.

2. **In-context examples help sub-linearly — tune k empirically.** "From Words to Numbers" shows cumulative regret grows sub-linearly in the number of in-context `(x, y)` pairs (diminishing but consistent gains), and Garg et al. explain why in-context learning behaves like function fitting. FF4 should sweep the number of retrieved neighbors k and expect a plateau; retrieve by config similarity so the Lipschitz-continuous embedding structure (Understanding LLM Embeddings) makes neighbors informative.

3. **Get uncertainty from samples/densities, not verbalized confidence.** LLM Processes and LLMTime obtain calibrated predictive distributions by sampling numeric completions; the UQ survey warns that RLHF models' stated confidences are overconfident (ECE ≥ 0.30, verbalized values bunched at 80–100%). For FF4, produce distributions by sampling and report calibration explicitly (ECE for the OOM flag, interval coverage for numeric predictions).

4. **Multi-task/shared training and rich metadata drive transfer.** OmniPred's multi-task model roughly halves error versus single-task, and anonymizing metadata more than doubles error (0.19 → 0.44). FF4 should train/serve one WM across ML-memory, vLLM-throughput, and FPGA domains and include human-readable field names and task descriptions in the prompt, not just raw numeric config values.

5. **Validate the failure boundary specifically, and position FF4 as an estimator feeding an optimizer.** The 2026 HPO study identifies OOM-avoidance as exactly where LLM search underperforms classical methods and notes LLMs poorly track state — so FF4 must feed history explicitly and be evaluated on OOM-boundary accuracy (precision/recall on the flag), not only average numeric error. The strongest published role for an LLM surrogate (LLAMBO, Centaur) is as a warmstart/pre-run estimator inside a classical loop, matching FF4's pre-run-estimator framing.

6. **Metrics conventions to adopt.** This literature reports *range-normalized MAE per task, clipped at 1.0* and aggregated across tasks (OmniPred); comparison against GP / Random Forest / MLP / Gradient-Boosting baselines (OmniPred, Vacareanu); and for probabilistic outputs, likelihood/CRPS-style density metrics plus calibration (LLM Processes, Chronos). FF4 should report per-domain normalized MAE against a GP baseline for the numeric head and ECE + precision/recall for the OOM flag.
## Specialized predictors: ML systems & EDA

FF4 proposes to use a trace-trained LLM world model as a pre-run estimator for ML-training memory/throughput, LLM-serving latency/throughput/KV-fit, and FPGA place-and-route (P&R) QoR. Each of those three domains already has a mature line of *specialized* predictors — analytical formulas, operator-level simulators, and learned regressors. This section surveys the ones that set the accuracy bar we must beat or honestly cite. A recurring theme: the best specialized predictors reach single-digit-percent error, but they buy that accuracy with domain-specific feature engineering and hardware-specific profiling, and they degrade exactly where FF4 hopes to win (unseen ops, unseen hardware, config space corners).

**Paleo: A Performance Model for Deep Neural Networks (ICLR 2017, [openreview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=SyVVJ85lg) · [code](https://talwalkarlab.github.io/paleo/))**
Paleo is a pure analytical model: it extracts the FLOP/byte requirements declared by a network architecture and maps them onto a design space of hardware FLOP/s, memory bandwidth, parallelization strategy, and communication scheme to predict training/inference time and scalability. No learning and no profiling — it is a first-principles roofline-style calculator. It was validated against then-current distributed training setups (e.g., AlexNet/VGG scaling).
Learnings for FF4:
- This is the canonical *analytical* baseline for training throughput/scaling; cite it as the "no-data, closed-form" lower bound FF4 must justify beating.
- Its inputs are exactly the config knobs FF4 will see (arch dims, device peak FLOP/s, parallelism), so it is a fair, cheap head-to-head baseline for domain 1.
- Its weakness is precisely trace-learned WM's opportunity: analytical roofline ignores kernel-level inefficiency, framework overhead, and memory fragmentation, so its throughput numbers are optimistic on real runs.

**Habitat / DeepView.Predict: A Runtime-Based Computational Performance Predictor for DNN Training (USENIX ATC 2021, [paper](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc21/presentation/yu) · [code](https://github.com/CentML/DeepView.Predict))**
Habitat predicts per-iteration training time on a *target* GPU you do not own, by profiling the iteration on a GPU you *do* own and scaling each operation across architectures via "wave scaling" (an execution-model-based analytical scale) or pre-trained MLPs for operations that don't scale simply. It reports 11.8% average iteration-time error across ResNet-50, Inception v3, Transformer, GNMT, and DCGAN over six GPU architectures.
Learnings for FF4:
- Sets a concrete cross-GPU transfer bar: ~12% error is achievable *with* one real profiling run on a reference GPU. FF4's promise (predict from traces without a fresh profile) is only compelling if it approaches this without the reference run.
- Its hybrid design (analytical scaling for regular kernels, learned MLP for irregular ones) is a useful architectural prior — pure-learned or pure-analytical each has gaps.
- Its Achilles heel is exactly cross-hardware generalization to architectures not in the scaling model, and it needs op-level profiling data; it is a strong, citable baseline for domain 1, not a strawman.

**Daydream: Accurately Estimating the Efficacy of Optimizations for DNN Training (USENIX ATC 2020, [paper](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc20/presentation/zhu-hongyu) · [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03318))**
Daydream builds a fine-grained kernel dependency graph from low-level CUPTI traces, then answers counterfactual "what if I apply optimization X?" questions by applying graph-transformation primitives (fuse, remove, scale, overlap) and re-simulating. It models most mainstream training optimizations (mixed precision, fusion, distribution/overlap) and predicts which will actually help on a given config.
Learnings for FF4:
- This is the closest prior in spirit to a "world model of a run": it is literally a trace-driven simulator answering counterfactuals. Cite it as the specialized incumbent for the "predict effect of a config change" use case.
- It demonstrates the value of the OTel-trace-as-input framing FF4 uses, but does so with a hand-built dependency-graph simulator rather than a learned model — the direct comparison FF4 should make.
- It requires a real profiled trace of the *baseline* run and hard-codes optimization semantics as graph rewrites; it cannot extrapolate to configs whose kernels never appeared in the trace, which is where a learned WM could generalize.

**DNNMem / Estimating GPU Memory Consumption of Deep Learning Models (ESEC/FSE 2020, [pdf](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/dnnmem.pdf) · [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3368089.3417050))**
DNNMem is an analytical GPU-memory estimator that walks the computation graph and sums in-use tensors, newly allocated tensors per op, plus CUDA-context and allocator-reservation overhead — the last being what naive "sum of tensor sizes" formulas miss. Evaluated across TensorFlow, PyTorch, and MXNet on five representative models, it closes much of the gap to measured peak memory.
Learnings for FF4:
- The direct analytical baseline for FF4's OOM/memory-fit prediction (domain 1). It shows that framework runtime overhead and allocator behavior are the hard part of memory prediction, not the tensor arithmetic.
- Its per-framework, per-allocator modeling is brittle: new frameworks, new allocators (e.g., PyTorch caching-allocator changes), and fragmentation break it — a learned model over traces could absorb allocator drift automatically.
- Cite it as the honest baseline for "will this config OOM"; FF4 should report both peak-memory error and OOM classification accuracy against it.

**DNNPerf / Runtime Performance Prediction for Deep Learning Models with Graph Neural Network (ICSE-SEIP 2023, [pdf](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/dnnperf.pdf) · [IEEE](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10172674/))**
DNNPerf is a *learned* predictor: it encodes the model as a DAG with node/edge features capturing computational semantics and uses an attention-based node-edge GNN encoder to predict both GPU memory and training-step time. It reports 13.7% error on memory and 7.4% on training time across thousands of real and synthetic configs, beating prior analytical and ML baselines.
Learnings for FF4:
- The strongest learned-regressor baseline for domain 1, and the fairest apples-to-apples comparison: it predicts the *same two targets* (memory, step time) FF4 targets, from a graph representation, using ML. FF4 must at least match ~7%/~14%.
- It validates GNN-over-computation-graph as the specialized SOTA design; FF4's pitch is that a single LLM WM over traces subsumes this without per-target feature engineering.
- Its features are hand-designed and its training set is synthetic-heavy; distribution shift to production configs and novel op types is the documented failure mode and FF4's opening.

**EleutherAI "Transformer Math 101" + cookbook calculators (blog, 2023, [post](https://blog.eleuther.ai/transformer-math/) · [code](https://github.com/EleutherAI/cookbook/tree/main/calc))**
Not a research system but the de-facto practitioner analytical formulas: training compute `C ≈ 6·P·D`, per-layer forward FLOPs `24·B·s·h² + 4·B·s²·h`, and additive memory budgets (weights + optimizer states + gradients + activations, divided by the relevant parallelism degrees). The cookbook ships runnable Python scripts for FLOP and device-memory estimates.
Learnings for FF4:
- This is the zero-cost baseline every reviewer will mentally apply; FF4 must show meaningful accuracy gains over these back-of-envelope formulas to justify a learned WM at all.
- The formulas are a good *sanity feature/prior* to feed the WM (or to residual-model against) rather than a competitor — predicting the delta from the analytical estimate is often easier than predicting the absolute.
- They systematically ignore realized MFU, kernel/library efficiency, fragmentation, and overlap, so they misprice exactly the runtime effects trace data captures — the empirical case for FF4.

**Vidur: A Large-Scale Simulation Framework for LLM Inference (MLSys 2024, [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05465) · [pdf](https://apanwariisc.github.io/publications/mlsys-2024-vidur/vidur_mlsys24.pdf) · [code](https://github.com/microsoft/vidur))**
Vidur is the most directly competitive system for FF4 domain 2. It models each LLM operator by profiling a *minimal set of representative input sizes* and then fitting a **random-forest regressor** (chosen over MLPs deliberately, for data frugality and to capture non-linear CUDA-kernel behavior that polynomial regression misses) to interpolate runtimes for unprofiled sizes; prefill and decode are modeled separately because their cost curves differ. It composes these operator estimates under real batching/scheduling policies (vLLM, Orca, FasterTransformer, Sarathi-Serve) to estimate end-to-end latency and throughput, achieving **<9% latency error** across its range. Its companion Vidur-Search finds the cheapest deployment config meeting SLOs for LLaMA2-70B in ~1 hour of CPU vs. an estimated 42K GPU-hours (~$218K) for real sweeps; both are open-source with a bundled workload-trace collection.
Learnings for FF4:
- This is the primary baseline for domain 2 and cannot be dismissed: it already predicts serving latency/throughput under real schedulers to <9% error, and it already does the "find best config cheaply" task FF4 markets. Beating or matching Vidur, on its own metrics and configs, is the credibility test.
- Vidur's per-hardware operator profiling is its cost *and* its ceiling: it must re-profile for each new GPU/model-op, and it assumes the scheduler is modeled explicitly. FF4's differentiator is predicting *without* re-profiling and generalizing across schedulers/hardware seen only in traces — that is the experiment to run head-to-head.
- Its random-forest-over-profiled-kernels design is strong empirical evidence that low-data, non-neural regressors are hard to beat on operator timing; FF4 should be prepared to show LLM-WM wins come from composition/generalization, not raw per-kernel accuracy.
- Its open workload traces and code are a directly usable evaluation harness and a candidate corpus to distill/compare against.

**LLMCompass: A Hardware Evaluation Framework for LLM Inference (ISCA 2024, [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03134) · [IEEE](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10609604/))**
LLMCompass is an architecture-level analytical/simulation framework for evaluating *hardware designs* (compute, memory hierarchy, HBM vs. DRAM) on LLM inference, with a mapper that searches tiling/scheduling and an area-based cost model. It reports ~4.1% average error on end-to-end LLM inference and ~10.4% per-operator, and can simulate a 4×A100 GPT-3-175B node in ~16 minutes on commodity hardware.
Learnings for FF4:
- Establishes that a *hardware-parameterized* analytical model hits ~4% inference error — a very high bar. If FF4 predicts serving performance, this is the strongest analytical competitor for domain 2 and a fair citation.
- Its strength (arbitrary hypothetical hardware) is orthogonal to FF4's (real, already-deployed hardware seen in traces); position FF4 as complementary for existing fleets rather than claiming to replace design-space HW exploration.
- It requires an explicit hardware spec and mapper search; it does not learn from production traces, so it cannot capture real-world serving noise, contention, or scheduler quirks — FF4's empirical territory.

**GenZ / Demystifying AI Platform Design for Distributed LLM Inference (arXiv 2406.01698, 2024, [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01698) · [code](https://github.com/abhibambhaniya/genz-llm-analyzer))**
GenZ is a lightweight *analytical* LLM-inference model spanning model families (dense, GQA, MoE, Mamba), serving optimizations (chunking, speculative decoding, quantization), and platform/parallelism configs to emit latency and memory estimates. It reports a max geomean error of ~5.82% vs. real hardware at roughly 30 ms per forward-pass estimate on an 8-core CPU.
Learnings for FF4:
- Another sub-6% analytical baseline for domain 2, and notably *fast and closed-form* — reinforces that the "cheap estimator" niche is already crowded with accurate specialized tools.
- Its coverage of MoE/Mamba/quantization is a checklist of model variants FF4 must handle to be competitive; use it to define the config axes for evaluation.
- Purely analytical and memory/latency-focused; it does not model tail latency, real queueing, or KV-cache eviction dynamics under load, which trace-based FF4 could.

**Ansor: Generating High-Performance Tensor Programs for Deep Learning (OSDI 2020, [paper](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi20/presentation/zheng) · [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06762))**
Ansor (now in Apache TVM) is the canonical *learned compiler cost model*: it samples tensor-program schedules from a hierarchical search space and uses a learned cost model (gradient-boosted / MLP over program features) plus evolutionary search to rank candidates, yielding up to 3.8×/2.6×/1.7× speedups on Intel/ARM/NVIDIA over prior art.
Learnings for FF4:
- The reference point for "learned model ranks configs" — but note the cost model is used for *relative ranking* within autotuning, not absolute latency prediction. If FF4 aims at config selection, ranking metrics (top-k, Kendall/Spearman) matter as much as absolute error; adopt Ansor's evaluation framing.
- Learned cost models here are cheap, retrained per hardware, and only need to be *monotone in the right order*; this is a lower-stakes accuracy regime than FF4's absolute-estimate pitch.
- Cite as adjacent prior art (compiler-level, not run-level), a fair analogy rather than a direct baseline.

**A Learned Performance Model for Tensor Processing Units + TpuGraphs (MLSys 2021 / NeurIPS 2023, [MLSys pdf](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2021/file/6bcfac823d40046dca25ef6d6d59cc3f-Paper.pdf) · [TpuGraphs arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13490) · [dataset](https://github.com/google-research-datasets/tpu_graphs))**
Kaufman et al. learn a GNN performance model over XLA computation graphs to predict TPU runtime, and show it beats a heavily hand-tuned analytical model on tile-size selection and operator fusion, and helps an autotuner when TPU access is scarce. The follow-on TpuGraphs is a large public dataset (graph + compile config + measured runtime) across ResNet/EfficientNet/Mask R-CNN/Transformer, explicitly built to train such models.
Learnings for FF4:
- Strongest evidence that GNN-over-compute-graph is the specialized SOTA for compiler/runtime prediction; the natural "specialized learned" baseline to contrast with an LLM-over-traces approach.
- **TpuGraphs is a directly reusable public sweep dataset** — thousands of (config → measured latency) samples FF4 could distill/train/evaluate on, or convert into traces. Flag it as a concrete data asset.
- These models predict runtime *given a static graph + compile config*; they don't ingest runtime traces and are TPU/XLA-specific, so transfer to arbitrary GPU workloads is unproven — FF4's generalization claim is untested here and worth an explicit experiment.

**Neo & Bao: Learned Query Optimizers (Neo, VLDB 2019, [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.14778/3342263.3342644); Bao, arXiv 2004.03814, [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03814) · [code](https://github.com/learnedsystems/BaoForPostgreSQL))**
Included as a cross-domain analogy for "learned model predicts execution cost to steer decisions." Neo learns plan cost end-to-end with a tree-CNN value network; Bao instead *steers* an existing optimizer with per-query hints via tree-CNN + Thompson sampling, learning online and adapting to workload/schema drift while cutting tail latency.
Learnings for FF4:
- Bao's lesson is the most transferable: pairing a learned predictor with a robust incumbent (hint the optimizer rather than replace it) beats a from-scratch learned model on reliability and tail behavior. FF4 should consider a *residual/steering* posture over analytical baselines (Paleo/GenZ) rather than pure replacement.
- Neo's cautionary tale — expensive to train, brittle under distribution shift, hard to trust in production — mirrors the risks reviewers will raise about an LLM WM; cite it honestly.
- Keep to at most these two; deeper DB-optimizer coverage is out of scope and a strawman for FF4's systems/EDA claims.

**HLS QoR Estimation: Fast and Accurate Estimation of Quality of Results in HLS with ML (FCCM 2018, [pdf](https://www.csl.cornell.edu/~zhiruz/pdfs/hls-qor-fccm2018.pdf) · [IEEE](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8457644/))**
Dai et al. train ML regressors (XGBoost, ANN) on features extracted from fast HLS reports to predict *post-implementation* FPGA resource usage and timing, closing the well-known gap between optimistic HLS estimates and true post-P&R QoR without running the slow implementation flow. On a 1,300-sample, 65-design corpus (CHStone, MachSuite, S2CBench), XGBoost cuts estimation error into the ~7–20% range and generalizes across FPGA device families.
Learnings for FF4:
- The canonical, most-cited specialized baseline for FF4 domain 3 (FPGA resource/timing). It defines both the task (report-features → true QoR) and the accuracy bar (~single-to-low-double-digit % error).
- Its features come from the *HLS tool's own report* — the analog of FF4's traces. FF4 must show that trace-based prediction adds value over these already-available intermediate estimates.
- It is design-suite-specific and struggles to generalize to unseen application classes / newer devices; cross-design and cross-device transfer is the documented weakness and FF4's target. Related public HLS datasets (HLSDataset, HLSFactory) exist for training/distillation.

**ML-for-EDA pre-P&R prediction: CongestionNet (routing congestion) & TimingPredict (pre-routing slack) (CongestionNet 2019; TimingPredict, DAC 2022, [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3489517.3530597) · [code](https://github.com/TimingPredict/TimingPredict))**
These are the most-cited GNN predictors that estimate final-implementation QoR from an *early* design stage. CongestionNet predicts detailed routing-congestion hotspots per cell from the gate-level netlist alone (pre-placement). TimingPredict is a timing-engine-inspired GNN that predicts pre-routing slack/arrival time, reporting net-delay and arrival-time correlations around 0.95 and 0.91 and using edge delays as auxiliary training targets.
Learnings for FF4:
- These set the domain-3 "pre-P&R timing/routability" bar: correlations ~0.9+ against post-route ground truth are achievable with purpose-built GNNs. FF4's P&R resource/timing estimates should report correlation and error against these, not just against the tool's own optimistic estimate.
- The recurring EDA lesson — encode circuit structure explicitly (netlist graph, timing arcs) — suggests FF4 loses information if it flattens P&R traces into generic sequences; structure-aware features may be needed to compete.
- Both need per-technology training data and degrade on unseen process nodes / design styles; that transfer gap is common across all specialized predictors and is FF4's consistent opening.

**CircuitNet: An Open-Source Dataset for ML Applications in EDA (arXiv 2208.01040, 2022, [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.01040) · [code/data](https://github.com/circuitnet/CircuitNet))**
Not a predictor but the key public dataset for domain 3: >10–20K samples from commercial-tool runs over open-source RISC-V designs, with labels for routability/congestion, IR-drop, and timing across back-end stages. It exists specifically because license restrictions kept ML-for-EDA data private.
Learnings for FF4:
- **A directly usable public sweep dataset** for FPGA/ASIC QoR prediction — candidate for training or, more importantly, for building the trace corpus and running honest baselines (CongestionNet/TimingPredict/HLS-QoR) FF4 must beat.
- Its cross-stage labels let FF4 test the pre-run-estimator claim end-to-end (early features → final QoR) on the same data the specialized SOTA uses.
- It is ASIC-back-end-flavored (RISC-V, commercial P&R); pair it with HLS datasets above if FF4's domain-3 story is FPGA/HLS specifically.

### Takeaways for FF4

1. **The bar is single-digit-percent, and it is already met by specialized tools.** Vidur (<9% latency), LLMCompass (~4% inference), GenZ (~5.8%), Habitat (~12% cross-GPU), DNNPerf (~7% time / ~14% memory), and HLS-QoR (~7–20%) all reach this with domain-specific methods. FF4 will not win on raw single-config accuracy; its case must be *generalization without re-profiling* and *one model across all three domains*. Report error **and** ranking metrics (top-k / Spearman), since the config-selection use case (à la Ansor, Vidur-Search) often only needs correct ordering.

2. **Specialized predictors need one of two expensive inputs: hardware-specific profiling or hand-engineered structural features.** Vidur/Habitat/Daydream re-profile per hardware; DNNPerf/CongestionNet/TimingPredict hand-design graph features; DNNMem/Paleo/GenZ hard-code framework/hardware analytics. FF4's differentiator is ingesting generic OTel traces instead — so the load-bearing experiment is: *does the trace-trained WM match these predictors on their own configs while needing no fresh profile or bespoke feature set?*

3. **They break on cross-hardware transfer, new ops/schedulers, and unseen design classes.** Every entry degrades outside its training/profiling distribution: Habitat on unmodeled GPU architectures, Vidur on unprofiled kernels/schedulers, DNNMem on new allocators, HLS-QoR/TimingPredict on unseen designs or process nodes. These are precisely the regimes to benchmark FF4 in; a fair evaluation must include held-out hardware/ops, not just interpolation.

4. **Fair, citable baselines (must-compare):** *Domain 1* — DNNPerf (learned) and DNNMem/Paleo (analytical). *Domain 2* — Vidur (the primary competitor; use its open code + traces), plus LLMCompass/GenZ as analytical references. *Domain 3* — HLS-QoR (Dai et al.) for FPGA resource/timing, CongestionNet + TimingPredict for pre-P&R, all trainable/evaluable on CircuitNet and HLS datasets. Bao is a fair *design-philosophy* citation (steer, don't replace).

5. **Strawmen to avoid:** EleutherAI Transformer-Math formulas and pure roofline (Paleo) are too coarse to be "the" comparison — use them as sanity lower bounds or as input features/residual targets, not as the headline baseline. Neo (and deeper DB-optimizer work) is analogy only; positioning it as a systems/EDA competitor would be a strawman.

6. **Concrete data assets to distill or benchmark against:** TpuGraphs (TPU/XLA graph→runtime, thousands of samples), Vidur's bundled workload traces + open code, CircuitNet (>10–20K EDA runs with QoR labels), and public HLS corpora (CHStone/MachSuite/S2CBench via HLSDataset/HLSFactory). Reusing these lets FF4 run the incumbent predictors as honest baselines on identical data rather than self-reported numbers.

## Surrogates, Bayesian optimization & active learning

Expensive black-box optimization — where each evaluation costs minutes to hours of compute — is exactly the regime FF4 targets. This literature spans the response-surface/Kriging tradition, model-based sequential design (Bayesian optimization), multi-fidelity and bandit resource allocation, failure-aware acquisition, and the recent turn toward pretrained/transfer/LLM surrogates. The through-line for FF4: a world model trained on OTel traces is a *surrogate* competing against these methods, so it must beat them on the axes they are weakest — cold start, structured/heterogeneous spaces, crash regions, and transfer across tasks.

**Efficient Global Optimization of Expensive Black-Box Functions (Journal of Global Optimization, 1998, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1008306431147).** Jones, Schonlau & Welch's EGO is the canonical recipe: fit a Kriging/GP surrogate to a space-filling initial design (Latin hypercube), then iteratively pick the next evaluation by maximizing Expected Improvement (EI), which balances exploiting the surrogate mean against exploring high-variance regions. EI (originally Mockus 1974) gives a closed-form, gradient-friendly acquisition and remains the default baseline 25+ years later. The GP's calibrated uncertainty is what makes it sample-efficient — the model *knows where it doesn't know*.
- **Learnings for FF4:** The EGO loop (surrogate → acquisition → evaluate → refit) is the exact skeleton of the "max batch that fits" demo; the WM plays the surrogate role.
- A WM must expose a *calibrated uncertainty estimate*, not just a point prediction, or it cannot drive a principled acquisition function — this is the single biggest thing a naive regressor lacks.
- Space-filling initialization (LHS/Sobol) is the standard cold-start; a WM's value proposition is skipping or shrinking this phase via priors.

**Practical Bayesian Optimization of Machine Learning Algorithms (NeurIPS 2012, https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2012/hash/05311655a15b75fab86956663e1819cd-Abstract.html).** Snoek, Larochelle & Adams brought BO to ML hyperparameter tuning and introduced *cost-aware* acquisition via Expected Improvement per second (EIPS): model the log wall-clock cost of each config with a second GP and divide EI by predicted cost, so the search prefers information-per-dollar rather than raw information. This is the origin of treating evaluation cost as first-class. Lee et al. (2020, https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.10870) later showed plain EIpu over-prefers cheap points and proposed "cost-cooling" (EI/c(x)^η with η annealed to 0), and Gittins-index variants (https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.20062) now give principled cost-aware acquisition.
- **Learnings for FF4:** FF4's measurement processes have wildly heterogeneous costs (a quick vLLM microbench vs. a full FPGA place-and-route taking hours) — cost-aware acquisition is essential, not optional, and the cost model itself is a thing the WM can predict.
- The WM can supply the cost surrogate *for free* (it already predicts runtime/latency), collapsing two GPs into one model.
- Cost-cooling's lesson: early search should be cheap-and-broad, late search expensive-and-targeted — mirror this in the demo's evaluation budget policy.

**Sequential Model-Based Optimization for General Algorithm Configuration / SMAC (LION 2011, https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-25566-3_40).** Hutter, Hoos & Leyton-Brown replaced the GP with a *random-forest* surrogate, which natively handles categorical, integer, and conditional (hierarchical) parameters and scales to many dimensions where GPs' cubic cost and stationarity assumptions break. SMAC is the workhorse behind auto-sklearn/Auto-WEKA and remains the go-to for structured configuration spaces. TPE (Bergstra et al. 2011) is the other non-GP mainstay, modeling p(x|y) with density ratios.
- **Learnings for FF4:** FF4 config spaces are heterogeneous and structured (model choice × batch size × dtype × hardware SKU × parallelism) — this is SMAC/RF territory, *not* vanilla GP territory. A WM ingesting traces inherits this structure naturally.
- Tree/RF surrogates give a cheap, robust baseline the WM must beat; use SMAC as the "classic surrogate" comparison point in FF4 experiments.

**Bayesian Optimization with Unknown Constraints (UAI 2014, https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~rpa/pubs/gelbart2014constraints.pdf).** Gelbart, Snoek & Adams formalized the case where feasibility is a black box discovered only by evaluating: they weight EI by the GP-predicted probability of feasibility (EIC = EI(x)·Pr[feasible(x)]), fitting a separate classifier-GP to the feasible/infeasible boundary. This directly models "crash regions." Follow-ups handle the pathologies: Predictive Entropy Search with constraints (Hernández-Lobato et al. 2015, http://proceedings.mlr.press/v37/hernandez-lobatob15.pdf) for decoupled/partial observations, and "crash-robust"/hidden-constraint BO where a run returns *no objective value at all* (https://hal.science/hal-04673615v1/file/s00158-024-03833-8.pdf).
- **Learnings for FF4:** OOM is precisely an *unknown-constraint crash region* — the run dies and returns no throughput number, only "infeasible." EIC's formulation (separate feasibility classifier × objective EI) is the right template for "max batch that fits."
- The WM should predict *P(OOM)* as an explicit head, not fold it into a regression target; the "max that fits" objective is literally finding the feasibility boundary, so boundary-exploration acquisition (https://arxiv.org/html/2402.07692v2) is directly applicable.
- Crashes are informative, not wasted: a failed real run is a labeled point on the OOM cliff and should be fed back into the WM.

**Multi-Fidelity Bayesian Optimisation with Continuous Approximations / BOCA (ICML 2017, https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06240) and Multi-Fidelity Cost-Aware BO (CMAME 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.02732).** Multi-fidelity BO exploits cheap, biased approximations (fewer epochs, smaller data, coarser mesh) correlated with the expensive high-fidelity target, spending most of the budget at low fidelity and only confirming promising points at full cost. Kandasamy's BOCA treats fidelity as a continuous knob; recent work adds cost-aware acquisition that maximizes improvement-per-unit-cost across fidelity levels, and deep-kernel/deep-NN variants (https://users.cs.utah.edu/~zhe/pdf/DeepMFBO.pdf) capture nonlinear cross-fidelity relationships.
- **Learnings for FF4:** The WM *is* a low-fidelity source — a near-free, biased estimate of an expensive measurement. Framing WM-vs-real-run explicitly as a two-fidelity (or multi-fidelity) problem gives a principled acquisition for *when to spend a real run*.
- Multi-fidelity theory quantifies the value of a biased-but-cheap surrogate as a function of its correlation with ground truth — this is the metric FF4 should report for its WM (fidelity/correlation, not just raw error).

**Freeze-Thaw Bayesian Optimization (arXiv 2014, https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.3896) and In-Context Freeze-Thaw BO / ifBO (ICML 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16795).** Freeze-thaw (Swersky, Snoek & Adams) is grey-box BO for iterative training: pause ("freeze") unpromising runs and resume ("thaw") promising ones, using a GP over *partial learning curves* with an exponential-decay kernel to extrapolate final performance. ifBO replaces the slow deep-GP surrogate with FT-PFN, a prior-data-fitted transformer that does Bayesian learning-curve extrapolation in one forward pass — 10–100× faster and state-of-the-art on deep-learning HPO.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Any FF4 target that is itself an iterative measurement (throughput ramping to steady state, a P&R run's intermediate timing reports) is a learning curve the WM can extrapolate — predict the endpoint from a truncated real run.
- ifBO shows the modern move: replace an expensive per-step-refit GP with a *pretrained amortized* surrogate — the same architectural bet FF4 makes with a trace-trained WM.

**Hyperband (JMLR 2018, https://jmlr.org/papers/v18/16-558.html), BOHB (ICML 2018, https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.01774), ASHA (MLSys 2020, https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.05934).** Hyperband (Li et al.) frames HPO as pure-exploration infinite-armed bandit: sample many random configs, allocate a small resource budget, keep the top fraction (successive halving), repeat across brackets — giving order-of-magnitude speedups with almost no modeling. BOHB replaces the random sampling with a TPE model for better late-stage convergence while keeping Hyperband's anytime behavior; ASHA makes successive halving asynchronous for massively parallel clusters (no synchronization stragglers).
- **Learnings for FF4:** These are the strong *model-free* baselines. If a WM cannot beat Hyperband/ASHA's sample efficiency, it isn't earning its keep — include them as the "no surrogate" comparison.
- BOHB's design lesson — model-based proposals *on top of* a bandit backbone — is a good architecture for the FF4 demo: use the WM to seed/rank configs, but keep a bandit-style budget allocator so a wrong WM can't waste the whole budget.
- ASHA's async point matters if FF4 ever runs real measurements in parallel on a cluster.

**Surrogate-Assisted Evolutionary Computation: Recent Advances and Future Challenges (Swarm and Evolutionary Computation, 2011, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2210650211000198); survey update "Evolutionary Computation for Expensive Optimization" (Machine Intelligence Research 2022, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11633-022-1317-4).** Jin's line of work embeds cheap surrogates (RBFs, GPs, RF, NNs) inside evolutionary algorithms to pre-screen candidate populations, only sending a filtered subset to the expensive true evaluation — "model management" decides which/when to evaluate for real. This community pioneered offline/data-driven optimization (build the surrogate once from a fixed dataset, then optimize against it with no further real evals) — structurally identical to FF4's "train WM from traces, then optimize."
- **Learnings for FF4:** "Model management" is the general name for the policy FF4 needs: given a surrogate of uncertain quality, decide which candidates deserve a real run. Borrow their taxonomy (individual- vs. generation-based control).
- The *offline* data-driven setting is FF4's exact setting when no new real runs are allowed — their warning: surrogate over-exploitation into inaccurate regions is the dominant failure mode, mitigated with surrogate ensembles and local trust regions.

**PFNs4BO: In-Context Learning for Bayesian Optimization (ICML 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17535).** Müller, Feurer, Hollmann & Hutter train a transformer (a Prior-Data Fitted Network) once on synthetic functions sampled from a chosen prior, then use it as a drop-in surrogate that produces a posterior predictive distribution in a single forward pass — no per-step GP refit. Priors (including hyperparameter-importance and user beliefs) are baked in at meta-training time, so the surrogate encodes structural knowledge a fresh GP lacks.
- **Learnings for FF4:** This is the closest published analogue to the FF4 WM: an *amortized, pretrained* surrogate replacing per-run GP inference. Its existence validates the approach and sets the bar.
- Key differentiator FF4 can claim: PFNs4BO is trained on *synthetic* priors, whereas the FF4 WM trains on *real OTel traces* — real provenance and real cross-config correlations the synthetic prior cannot manufacture.

**Large Language Models to Enhance Bayesian Optimization / LLAMBO (ICLR 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03921).** Liu, Astorga, Seedat & van der Schaar frame the whole BO loop in natural language so an LLM can (a) zero-shot *warm-start* from a task description, (b) act as the surrogate, and (c) propose candidates — no fine-tuning, modular so each piece drops into an existing BO stack. Gains are largest exactly where GPs are weakest: the early, data-sparse regime, because the LLM injects prior domain knowledge from pretraining.
- **Learnings for FF4:** This is direct evidence that *language priors buy cold-start sample efficiency* — the core argument for why a WM/LLM beats a from-scratch GP on the first handful of points.
- LLAMBO's modularity is a template: FF4 need not replace the whole BO stack; slotting the WM in as the surrogate and/or warm-start proposer is a legitimate, evaluable contribution.
- Caveat to test: LLM/WM surrogate quality can be miscalibrated; LLAMBO keeps a real acquisition loop as a safety net rather than trusting the LLM blindly — FF4 should keep real runs in the loop for the same reason.

**HPO-B (NeurIPS Datasets & Benchmarks 2021, https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06257) and YAHPO Gym (AutoML Conf 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.03670).** Two reusable, tabular/surrogate benchmark suites for *transfer* and *multi-fidelity* HPO. HPO-B is 176 search spaces × 196 OpenML datasets × 6.4M real evaluations, with explicit train/test splits and protocols for both non-transfer and transfer settings. YAHPO Gym ships ~700 surrogate-model instances across 14 scenarios, every one queryable at reduced fidelity and returning multiple objectives — built specifically to make transfer- and multi-fidelity-HPO experiments fast and reproducible.
- **Learnings for FF4:** These give FF4 a ready-made, no-real-compute testbed to validate the WM-as-surrogate idea *and* the transfer story before touching expensive vLLM/FPGA runs.
- Their design pattern — a surrogate *of* a benchmark, with held-out task splits — is exactly how FF4 should evaluate cross-task transfer of the WM (train on some workloads, test cold on unseen ones).

### Takeaways for FF4

1. **Failure regions are the main event, and crashes are labels.** OOM is an unknown/hidden constraint (crash-robust BO, Gelbart-style EIC): the run returns no number, only "infeasible." Model *P(crash)* as an explicit head, treat every failed real run as a labeled point on the cliff, and use boundary-exploration acquisition — "max batch that fits" is literally finding the feasibility boundary, so the WM should be optimized to localize that boundary, not to minimize average regression error across the whole space.

2. **Sample-efficiency expectations at ~300 points.** 300 evaluations is comfortable for GP-based BO (GPs shine at 10²–10³ points before cubic cost bites) and generous for Hyperband/BOHB/SMAC. So a WM that needs a real run per query offers little over a GP at this scale — the WM's payoff must come from *reducing real runs well below 300* via transfer/priors, or from operating where GPs structurally fail (heterogeneous/conditional spaces → use SMAC/RF or a WM, not a vanilla GP). Benchmark the WM against SMAC, BOHB/ASHA, and a GP, and report *real runs to target*, not iterations.

3. **Sweep design: space-filling to seed, adaptive to finish.** The field's settled practice is Latin-hypercube/Sobol for the initial design (unbiased coverage, good for fitting the surrogate globally) then adaptive/model-driven acquisition (EI, EIC, cost-aware) once the surrogate is trustworthy. FF4's differentiator is shrinking or skipping the space-filling phase: a trace-trained WM should provide a warm start (LLAMBO/PFN-style) so the first "real" batch is already informed, and the adaptive phase should be cost-aware (EIps/cost-cooling) given FF4's huge per-eval cost spread.

4. **Frame the WM explicitly as a fidelity in a multi-fidelity loop.** The cleanest, most defensible framing: the WM is a near-free low-fidelity source, real measurement is high-fidelity, and multi-fidelity BO tells you *when to pay for a real run* based on the WM's predicted correlation with ground truth. Report the WM's fidelity/correlation as the headline metric, and keep real runs in the loop as a safety net against surrogate over-exploitation (the dominant failure mode in offline/data-driven optimization).

5. **What a WM must offer beyond a GP to be worth using.** (a) *Calibrated uncertainty* — without it there is no valid acquisition function; this is table stakes, not a bonus. (b) *Priors from language/pretraining* — cold-start warm-starting is where LLAMBO/PFNs show the clearest win over from-scratch GPs, and it's FF4's strongest argument. (c) *Transfer across tasks* — validated cheaply on HPO-B/YAHPO before expensive runs; the WM should generalize to unseen workloads/hardware zero-shot. (d) *Provenance from real traces* — unlike PFNs4BO (synthetic priors) or a GP (no priors), the WM encodes real cross-config correlations and cost structure observed in OTel traces, which is the one thing none of the classical surrogates can manufacture.

6. **Don't replace the loop, slot into it.** Every modern win (BOHB, ifBO, LLAMBO) keeps a proven optimization backbone and swaps in a better surrogate/proposer. FF4's lowest-risk, most-evaluable contribution is the WM as (i) cold-start proposer, (ii) surrogate/low-fidelity source, and (iii) crash predictor inside an otherwise standard cost-aware, constraint-aware BO/bandit loop — with model-free baselines (Hyperband/ASHA) and classic surrogates (GP-EGO, SMAC) as the yardsticks it must beat.
## Learned surrogates for physical systems & digital twins

Across weather, fluids, chip design, materials, and batteries, a consistent pattern has emerged over the last five years: where a ground-truth process is slow, expensive, or physically destructive, a learned surrogate trained on records of that process can stand in for it — as a fast forward predictor, a screening filter, or a pre-run estimator. This is the closest external analogue to what FF4 attempts (using a trace-trained world model to estimate a run's outcome before committing the real run), and the literature offers both proof that it works and hard-won cautions about where fidelity breaks.

**GraphCast: "Learning skillful medium-range global weather forecasting" (Science, 2023 — [science.org/10.1126/science.adi2336](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adi2336))**
DeepMind's message-passing GNN over a multi-scale spherical mesh predicts hundreds of atmospheric variables 10 days out at 0.25° resolution (>1M grid points) in under a minute on a single TPU v4, versus hours of supercomputer time for ECMWF's HRES numerical model. It outperformed HRES on more than 90% of 1,380 verification targets (99.7% within the troposphere) and improved severe-event prediction. It was trained on ~40 years of ECMWF ERA5 reanalysis. This is the flagship existence proof that a learned surrogate can beat a mature, physics-based simulator on its own turf.
Learnings for FF4:
- The winning recipe was a large, uniform, decades-deep corpus of the real process (ERA5), not clever architecture alone — FF4's bet on OTel traces as the training substrate is the same bet, and corpus depth is the lever.
- The value proposition is identical to FF4's: not necessarily *better* than the ground-truth process, but orders of magnitude cheaper/faster, which changes what you can afford to explore.
- GraphCast beats HRES on *most but not all* targets; a credible surrogate is characterized by *where* it holds, not a single headline accuracy — FF4 should report per-regime skill, not one number.

**MeshGraphNets: "Learning Mesh-Based Simulation with Graph Networks" (ICLR 2021 — [arxiv.org/abs/2010.03409](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03409))**
Pfaff et al. run message passing on a simulation mesh with adaptive remeshing during rollout, accurately modeling aerodynamics, structural mechanics, and cloth at 1–2 orders of magnitude faster than the solver it learned from. It generalizes to finer/larger meshes at test time than seen in training. This is the canonical demonstration that a learned simulator can capture continuum mechanics (structural, aerodynamic) — the exact physics behind FF4's target physical domains like board thermal and mechanical bring-up.
Learnings for FF4:
- Structure matters: encoding the problem's native topology (a mesh, or for FF4 a run's causal/dependency graph) is what enabled generalization beyond the training distribution.
- Generalizing to *larger* instances than trained on is the property FF4 most needs for physical hardware, where you can rarely afford training runs at production scale.

**Graph Network Simulators: "Learning to Simulate Complex Physics with Graph Networks" (ICML 2020 — [proceedings.mlr.press/v119/sanchez-gonzalez20a.html](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/sanchez-gonzalez20a.html))**
Sanchez-Gonzalez et al. represent physical state as particles-as-graph-nodes and learn one-step dynamics via message passing, handling fluids, rigid solids, and deformables together. Trained on single-timestep prediction, it stays stable over thousands of rollout steps and generalizes to an order of magnitude more particles. The paper isolates the two determinants of long-horizon stability: number of message-passing steps, and injecting training noise to curb compounding rollout error.
Learnings for FF4:
- Compounding error over long rollouts is the central failure mode of any autoregressive surrogate; if FF4 ever estimates multi-step trajectories rather than a single terminal outcome, the training-noise trick is directly applicable.
- Training on one-step prediction while evaluating on long rollouts is a data-efficiency pattern worth borrowing.

**Fourier Neural Operator: "Fourier Neural Operator for Parametric PDEs" (ICLR 2021 — [arxiv.org/abs/2010.08895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08895))**
Li et al. learn the *solution operator* of a PDE family directly in Fourier space, making the model mesh- and resolution-invariant (train at 64×64, evaluate at 256×256 zero-shot) and up to three orders of magnitude faster than conventional solvers; it was the first ML method to handle turbulent Navier–Stokes. The follow-on FourCastNet (Pathak et al., 2022 — [arxiv.org/abs/2202.11214](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.11214)) scaled adaptive FNOs to global 0.25° weather, matching IFS on large scales and beating it on precipitation, at a week-long forecast in under 2 seconds.
Learnings for FF4:
- Operator learning (map input *conditions* → output *field/outcome*) is a cleaner framing than step-by-step simulation when you only care about the end estimate — which is exactly FF4's pre-run-estimator use case.
- Resolution/config invariance is what lets one trained model serve many problem instances; FF4 should aim for a model that generalizes across configurations (batch sizes, board variants) rather than one model per setup.

**DeepONet: "Learning nonlinear operators via DeepONet" (Nature Machine Intelligence, 2021 — [nature.com/articles/s42256-021-00302-5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-021-00302-5))**
Lu et al. give operator learning a theoretical footing (the universal approximation theorem for operators) with a branch-net/trunk-net architecture that learns explicit and implicit operators for deterministic and stochastic ODEs/PDEs. It establishes that a single learned model can approximate broad classes of engineering operators. Together with FNO it defines the "neural operator" line that underpins learned FEA/CFD surrogacy.
Learnings for FF4:
- There is theory, not just empirics, behind "a network can stand in for a physical operator" — useful for the credibility of the FF4 thesis in a research writeup.
- The branch/trunk split (encode the *situation* separately from the *query point*) is a reusable design when FF4 must answer many queries about one configured run.

**AlphaChip: "A graph placement methodology for fast chip design" (Nature, 2021 — [nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03544-w](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03544-w))**
Mirhoseini et al. cast macro placement as RL over a graph-convolutional policy, producing floorplans in under six hours that are comparable or superior to human experts on power/performance/area, and used it in production across (per the 2024 AlphaChip addendum and blog) the last three TPU generations. Importantly, the result is genuinely contested: Cheng et al. (ISPD 2023, [arxiv.org/abs/2302.11014](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11014)) and Markov's CACM critique argue the open reproduction underperforms commercial tools and that undocumented pre-placement/pre-training steps carried much of the win; DeepMind rebutted ("That Chip Has Sailed," [arxiv.org/abs/2411.10053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10053)). Nature issued an addendum, not a retraction.
Learnings for FF4:
- This is FF4's nearest neighbor in spirit — a learned model replacing a slow physical/EDA design-iteration loop, and notably in *the same FPGA P&R adjacent domain* as FF4 v1. Cite it as precedent.
- The reproducibility fight is the cautionary tale: undisclosed preprocessing and unequal compute made the headline numbers unfalsifiable to outsiders. FF4 must publish the full pipeline, baselines under matched compute, and open reproductions, or invite the same skepticism.

**GNoME: "Scaling deep learning for materials discovery" (Nature, 2023 — [nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06735-9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06735-9))**
DeepMind's GNN, trained with active learning to predict crystal stability, proposed 2.2M new structures with ~380,000 predicted stable — described as roughly a 45× expansion over stable compounds found in prior history — screening candidates far faster than running DFT on each. External labs experimentally realized 736 of them, and ~380k were released into the Materials Project. (The DFT speedup is stated qualitatively, not as a single clean multiplier.) A critical perspective (Cheetham & Seshadri, *Chem. Mater.* 2024) questions how many candidates are genuinely novel/useful.
Learnings for FF4:
- GNoME is a surrogate used as a *screening filter* over a vast search space before spending the expensive resource (DFT / synthesis) — precisely FF4's "estimate cheaply, only then commit the real run" role.
- The novelty critique warns that "the surrogate flagged N candidates" is a weak claim; FF4's value must be measured in expensive-runs-avoided and downstream-confirmed hits, not raw prediction counts.

**A-Lab: "An autonomous laboratory for the accelerated synthesis of novel materials" (Nature, 2023 — [nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06734-w](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06734-w))**
Szymanski, Ceder et al. coupled Materials Project + GNoME stability data, literature-mined ML synthesis recipes, active learning, and robotic synthesis with automated XRD to make 41 of 58 target compounds in 17 days of continuous operation. It is the closed loop: cheap prediction → automated expensive experiment → feedback. It also drew a substantive critique (Leeman, Palgrave et al.) that many "novel" compounds were ordered versions of known disordered phases and that automated XRD refinement was unreliable; Nature published a correction in January 2026.
Learnings for FF4:
- The end state FF4's thesis points at: a surrogate that not only estimates but *chooses which real (physical) experiments to run*, closing the loop with the expensive process. Cite as the aspirational bridge.
- The critique is a validation lesson: automated interpretation of physical measurements (here XRD; for FF4, board telemetry / test logs) can be confidently wrong. FF4's ground-truth labels must be audited, not trusted because a pipeline produced them.

**Battery cycle-life prediction — the canonical expensive-measurement domain: Severson et al., "Data-driven prediction of battery cycle life before capacity degradation" (Nature Energy, 2019 — [nature.com/articles/s41560-019-0356-8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41560-019-0356-8)) and Attia et al., "Closed-loop optimization of fast-charging protocols" (Nature, 2020 — [nature.com/articles/s41586-020-1994-5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-1994-5))**
Severson cycled 124 commercial LFP/graphite cells to failure (lifetimes 150–2,300 cycles) and showed that discharge-voltage features from the *first 100 cycles* — before any measurable capacity fade — predict cycle life at 9.1% test error, and that the first *5 cycles* classify cells into high/low-life groups at 4.9% error. Because cycling a cell to death takes months, this collapses the measurement cost of the single most expensive quantity in battery R&D. Attia then closed the loop: pairing early prediction with Bayesian optimization, it found strong fast-charge protocols among 224 candidates in 16 days, versus an estimated 500+ days for exhaustive testing.
Learnings for FF4:
- This is the cleanest economic argument for FF4's entire thesis, and battery cycling is explicitly on FF4's physical roadmap. The frame — "predict the expensive terminal outcome from cheap early signal" — is *literally* FF4's pre-run estimator; borrow this narrative directly.
- The data was *deliberately generated* with a wide-spread design of experiments (150–2,300 cycles) so the model could learn the full failure envelope. FF4's physical corpus should be captured with the same intent: engineer diversity of outcomes, don't just log whatever runs happen.
- Attia proves the ROI: the surrogate's real payoff is in the *search* it unlocks (protocols/designs you can now afford to evaluate), not just point accuracy.
- Public dataset for a future WM corpus: the MIT/Stanford/Toyota 124-cell set at [data.matr.io/1/projects/5c48dd2bc625d700019f3204](https://data.matr.io/1/projects/5c48dd2bc625d700019f3204).

**NASA C-MAPSS turbofan prognostics / RUL: "Damage propagation modeling for aircraft engine run-to-failure simulation" (PHM 2008 — [ntrs.nasa.gov](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090029214/downloads/20090029214.pdf))**
Saxena et al.'s C-MAPSS dataset (sub-sets FD001–FD004) is the field-standard benchmark for Remaining Useful Life prediction, with deep models (CNN/LSTM/transformer) now standard on it. Crucially, it is *simulated* sensor data from a thermodynamic engine model, not physical run-to-failure tests — which is exactly why it exists: real run-to-failure data on jet engines is prohibitively expensive to collect. It anchors the entire prognostics-and-health-management (PHM) literature and is public via the NASA Open Data portal.
Learnings for FF4:
- Prognostics is a mature discipline built on exactly FF4's problem shape — predict a future failure/margin from run telemetry — and gives FF4 an established evaluation vocabulary (RUL, scoring functions that penalize late predictions more than early ones). Adopt PHM-style asymmetric scoring for safety-critical physical estimates.
- That the community's canonical benchmark is itself *simulated* is a double-edged precedent: usable as bootstrap data, but a reminder that a benchmark's realism ceiling is set by its data source. Public sets to seed a future WM corpus: C-MAPSS ([data.nasa.gov](https://data.nasa.gov/dataset/cmapss-jet-engine-simulated-data)) and the NASA PCoE battery-aging sets.

**Digital twins in manufacturing (surveys + the hype/reality gap): Kritzinger et al. (IFAC-PapersOnLine, 2018 — [doi.org/10.1016/j.ifacol.2018.08.474](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ifacol.2018.08.474)), Tao et al. (IEEE T-II, 2019 — [doi.org/10.1109/TII.2018.2873186](https://doi.org/10.1109/TII.2018.2873186)), and Kortelainen et al., "Digital twin—The dream and the reality" (Frontiers in IoT, 2023 — [doi.org/10.3389/friot.2023.1108777](https://doi.org/10.3389/friot.2023.1108777))**
Kritzinger gives the load-bearing taxonomy by data-integration level: a *digital model* (manual data flow), a *digital shadow* (automatic physical→virtual only), and a true *digital twin* (automated bidirectional coupling where the virtual state also acts on the physical). Tao is the high-citation state-of-the-art survey framing DT as an Industry-4.0 enabler. Kortelainen documents the reality gap honestly: term inflation by marketing, that most implementations lack real-time bidirectional coupling, that nine of ten studied "twins" existed only in labs, and a pervasive standardization/interoperability gap.
Learnings for FF4:
- Use Kritzinger's taxonomy to position FF4 precisely and avoid overclaiming: FF4 is a *learned surrogate / pre-run estimator*, not a live bidirectionally-coupled twin. Calling it a "digital twin" would inherit the credibility problems below.
- The digital-twin field's central disappointment — polished demos, few production deployments, no live data coupling — is the trap FF4 must visibly avoid by grounding in real OTel traces and reporting real deployment outcomes, not simulated ones.

**Waymo counterfactual safety simulation: Scanlon et al., "Waymo simulated driving behavior in reconstructed fatal crashes…" (Accident Analysis & Prevention, 2021 — [Waymo PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/waymo-uploads/files/documents/Waymo-Simulated-Driving-Behavior-in-Reconstructed-Collisions.pdf))**
With context from Waymo's SimulationCity (2021), the safety-methodology paper (Webb et al., [arxiv.org/abs/2011.00054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00054)), and sensor-realism work SurfelGAN (CVPR 2020, [arxiv.org/abs/2005.03844](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.03844)). Waymo reconstructed a census of 72 fatal human-involved crashes and, via counterfactual alignment, swapped the simulated Waymo Driver in for one human actor at a time: as the initiator it avoided every collision, and as the responder it prevented an estimated 82% and mitigated a further 10%. SimulationCity extends this to what-if variation of real logged events and generation of rare risky scenarios, all grounded in 20M+ real autonomous miles.
Learnings for FF4:
- This is the strongest template for FF4's *counterfactual* pitch: take a real logged run and re-simulate it with one variable changed ("what if this board revision / config / charge protocol"), which is exactly pre-run estimation for a design you have not built.
- Waymo's method is only credible because the counterfactuals are anchored to reconstructed *real* events — FF4's counterfactual estimates should likewise be tied to, and back-validated against, real traces, and the whole apparatus rests on sim realism (SurfelGAN) being explicitly measured.

**Sim-to-real gap: Tobin et al., "Domain Randomization…" (IROS 2017 — [arxiv.org/abs/1703.06907](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06907)), OpenAI et al., "Solving Rubik's Cube with a Robot Hand" (2019 — [arxiv.org/abs/1910.07113](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.07113)), surveyed in Zhao et al. (IEEE SSCI 2020 — [arxiv.org/abs/2009.13303](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.13303))**
Tobin showed a network trained purely on randomized, non-photorealistic simulation transferred to real robotic localization at ~1.5 cm accuracy; OpenAI's Automatic Domain Randomization auto-expands the randomization distribution until real-world control emerges (Rubik's cube on a real hand). The survey organizes the field's answers to the reality gap: randomization, domain adaptation, imitation, meta-learning, distillation. The unifying lesson is that surrogate/sim fidelity gaps are *the* cause of transfer failure, closed either by widening the sim to envelop reality or by calibrating against real data.
Learnings for FF4:
- FF4 will have a reality gap between trace-trained estimates and real physical runs; the mitigations are known — either broaden the training distribution to cover the deployment regime, or continuously recalibrate against a trickle of real ground-truth runs (system identification).
- ADR's insight — don't hand-tune the distribution, learn how wide it needs to be — suggests FF4 should actively measure and expand where its corpus is thin rather than assume coverage.

**2024–2026 foundation models for physical simulation & engineering design: Poseidon (NeurIPS 2024 — [arxiv.org/abs/2405.19101](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19101)), The Well (NeurIPS 2024 D&B — [github.com/PolymathicAI/the_well](https://github.com/PolymathicAI/the_well)), GenCast (Nature, 2024 — [doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08252-9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08252-9)), and "LLMs for Computer-Aided Design: A Survey" (2025 — [arxiv.org/abs/2505.08137](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08137))**
The frontier is generalization *across* physics: Poseidon is a multiscale operator transformer pretrained on fluid dynamics that transfers to 15 downstream PDE tasks including unseen physics, and The Well is the enabling ~15 TB, 16-domain public corpus of physics simulations. GenCast is a diffusion ensemble weather model beating ECMWF ENS on 97.2% of 1,320 targets — a domain-specific surrogate, not a general foundation model (as with NVIDIA's PhysicsNeMo *framework*, worth naming but not miscalling a foundation model). The LLM-CAD survey marks the nascent thread of language models predicting/generating engineering-design artifacts.
Learnings for FF4:
- The field is converging on FF4's structure — one model pretrained on diverse traces of physical processes, transferred to new tasks — which makes FF4's "world model for engineering runs" framing timely rather than speculative.
- The gating factor is again *data*: Poseidon needed The Well. FF4's analogous move is a large, diverse, openly-scoped OTel trace corpus; the physical-domain public sets named above (battery, C-MAPSS, The Well, Waymo Open) are realistic bootstrap inputs.
- Be precise in the writeup about "foundation model" vs "framework" vs "domain-specific surrogate" — reviewers of this space are, post-AlphaChip, sensitive to overclaiming.

### Takeaways for FF4

1. **What made surrogates succeed was data scale and matching structure, not architecture.** GraphCast/GenCast needed 40 years of ERA5; Poseidon needed The Well; Severson needed a deliberately diverse 124-cell design of experiments. Every success pairs a large corpus of the *real* process with a representation that matches the problem's native structure (mesh, graph, operator). FF4's leverage is therefore the OTel trace corpus — its depth, and how deliberately its outcome diversity is engineered — far more than the model class. Capture traces the way Severson cycled cells: to span the full outcome envelope, not just the common case.

2. **The physical-domain data-acquisition path is "expensive-but-finite, so bootstrap then close the loop."** Physical ground truth (a battery cycled to death, a board respin, a run-to-failure engine) is costly but not unavailable, and several public corpora already exist to start on (MIT/Stanford/Toyota battery, NASA C-MAPSS and PCoE battery-aging, Waymo Open, The Well). The proven pattern is A-Lab/Attia's closed loop: use the surrogate to *choose* which expensive physical runs to spend on, feed results back, and let coverage grow toward the regimes that matter. Plan FF4's physical corpus as an active-learning acquisition problem, not a one-time logging exercise.

3. **Safety-critical validation expects per-regime skill maps, asymmetric scoring, and back-validation against real events — not a single accuracy number.** GraphCast reports skill across 1,380 targets; PHM uses RUL scores that punish late (dangerous) predictions harder than early ones; Waymo grounds every counterfactual in a reconstructed real crash. For FF4's physical bridge, that means: publish where the estimator holds and where it doesn't, use asymmetric loss so an over-optimistic estimate (predicting "pass" when the real run would fail) costs more than the reverse, and validate counterfactual estimates against held-out real runs.

4. **The credibility failures in this literature are all overclaiming and irreproducibility — design against them explicitly.** AlphaChip's win is still disputed over undisclosed preprocessing and unmatched-compute baselines; A-Lab's "novel materials" and GNoME's "discovery" counts were challenged and corrected; the digital-twin field is distrusted because demos outran deployments. FF4 should ship the full pipeline, baselines under matched compute, an open reproduction path, and metrics stated as runs-avoided / real-hits-confirmed rather than raw prediction volume. Use Kritzinger's taxonomy to call FF4 a *learned surrogate/pre-run estimator*, not a "digital twin."

5. **Frame FF4 as operator-learning / early-signal prediction, and reserve "counterfactual" and "foundation model" for what they precisely mean.** FNO/DeepONet legitimize "learn the input-conditions → outcome operator" as the right abstraction for a pre-run estimator; Severson legitimizes "predict the expensive terminal outcome from cheap early signal"; Waymo legitimizes "re-run a real log with one variable changed." These are FF4's three honest verbs. Avoid calling domain-specific surrogates or frameworks (GenCast, PhysicsNeMo) foundation models, since this audience now watches for it.

6. **The FF4 → physical bridge paragraph writes itself from the strongest precedents.** Recommended framing: *"The economic case is established. Severson and Attia showed that predicting a battery's months-long cycle-life from its first few cycles turns a 500-day protocol search into a 16-day one; Waymo validates unbuilt driving behavior by re-simulating real fatal crashes with the AV swapped in; GNoME and A-Lab use cheap learned screening to decide which expensive syntheses are worth running. FF4 applies the same move — a model trained on real execution traces estimates a run's outcome before the run is committed — first on compute proxies (ML memory/OOM, vLLM, FPGA place-and-route, itself the domain of AlphaChip) where ground truth is cheap enough to build the method rigorously, then to physical hardware (PCB respins, board bring-up, battery cycling, thermal) where each verification is slow, expensive, and safety-critical, and where the surrogate's payoff — respins and test-cycles avoided — is largest."* Lead with the battery numbers and the counterfactual framing; anchor the compute→physical step on AlphaChip's adjacency to FF4 v1's FPGA P&R domain; and close on the validation discipline (per-regime skill, asymmetric scoring, real-run back-validation) so the bridge reads as engineering, not aspiration.
## Predicting experiment & research outcomes

Pre-run prediction of "how will this whole experiment/attempt turn out?" is emerging from two directions that FF4 sits between: (a) benchmarks that *measure* whether an agent can execute a research task end-to-end, which incidentally release the run logs needed to *train* an outcome predictor, and (b) work showing LLMs can forecast scientific and experimental results better than chance and sometimes better than experts. This section surveys both.

**MLE-bench: Evaluating Machine Learning Agents on Machine Learning Engineering (OpenAI, ICLR 2025 — [arXiv:2410.07095](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07095), [code](https://github.com/openai/mle-bench))**
75 Kaggle ML-engineering competitions with per-competition human baselines drawn from public Kaggle leaderboards; agents train models, prepare data, and submit. Best setup (o1-preview + AIDE scaffolding) reaches at least a bronze medal in 16.9% of competitions, and the paper studies resource-scaling and pre-training contamination. The harness is open-sourced but the abstract does not advertise a released corpus of *agent run logs* — those must be regenerated by running the (expensive) harness yourself.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - The natural label is graded and continuous (medal tier / percentile vs. leaderboard), which is exactly the shape of a pre-run outcome estimator target, not a binary pass/fail.
  - Each ground-truth point costs a full agent run on real Kaggle data (GPU hours), so a distilled corpus is expensive per point — argues for capturing OTel traces *while we run agents anyway* rather than a dedicated data-collection campaign.
  - Contamination is a first-class threat: competitions in pre-training inflate scores, so an outcome predictor must not just memorize "this task is easy."

**PaperBench: Evaluating AI's Ability to Replicate AI Research (OpenAI, ICML 2025 — [arXiv:2504.01848](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01848), [code](https://github.com/openai/preparedness))**
Agents replicate 20 ICML 2024 Spotlight/Oral papers from scratch (understand the paper, build the codebase, run the experiments). The key asset is a hierarchical rubric of 8,316 individually gradable leaf tasks co-developed with the original authors, plus an LLM judge and a separate judge-eval dataset to validate grading. Best agent (Claude 3.5 Sonnet New + open scaffolding) scores ~21.0% average replication; top ML PhDs still beat the models on the sampled subset.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - The rubric decomposition is a template for turning "did this experiment succeed?" into a dense, gradable signal instead of a single scalar — useful if we want the WM to predict *which parts* of an attempt will fail.
  - It demonstrates a validated LLM-judge-as-grader pipeline (with its own meta-eval), directly relevant to our RubricJudge/reward-judge seam.
  - Ground-truth is extremely expensive (multi-hour agent runs + author-built rubrics), so PaperBench is more valuable to us as a *held-out test set for a WM predictor* than as bulk training data.

**RE-Bench: Evaluating frontier AI R&D capabilities of LM agents against human experts (METR, 2024 — [arXiv:2411.15114](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15114), [blog](https://metr.org/blog/2024-11-22-evaluating-r-d-capabilities-of-llms/))**
Seven open-ended ML research-engineering environments (fit a scaling law, optimize a GPU kernel, etc.) with a released dataset of 71 eight-hour attempts by 61 human experts, each with time-stamped score trajectories. 82% of expert attempts get a non-zero score; agents beat humans 4x at a 2-hour budget but humans pull ahead with more time. This is the closest existing artifact to "predict the outcome of a research-engineering attempt."
- Learnings for FF4:
  - The released human *score-vs-time trajectories* are close to gold for pre-run/early-signal outcome prediction — you can ask "given the first N minutes, what's the final score?" at the experiment level.
  - Tasks map cleanly onto FF4's expensive-process framing (kernel optimization, scaling-law fitting) and give a human-calibrated y-axis.
  - Small n (7 environments) means it's a validation/transfer target, not a training corpus on its own.

**Large language models surpass human experts in predicting neuroscience results (Luo et al., Nature Human Behaviour 2024 — [arXiv:2403.03230](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03230), [BrainBench code](https://github.com/braingpt-lovelab/BrainBench))**
BrainBench presents 200 two-choice items where a real neuroscience abstract is paired with a plausibly-altered "wrong result" version; the task is to pick the true outcome. General LLMs beat 171 human experts (avg ~10 yrs experience), and BrainGPT (fine-tuned on 1.3B tokens of neuroscience literature) does better still. Crucially, model confidence was calibrated — high-confidence predictions were more likely correct.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - Strong existence proof that an LLM/WM can predict *experimental outcomes* from a description alone, before any run — and that domain fine-tuning helps, mirroring our "train the WM on real traces" thesis.
  - The calibration result (confidence tracks accuracy) is the property FF4 most needs from a pre-run estimator, and it was achievable here — a selective-prediction / abstain design is realistic.
  - The forced-choice framing (real vs. altered outcome) is a cheap way to build eval items from any corpus of real runs we already have.

**Approaching Human-Level Forecasting with Language Models (Halawi, Zhang, Yueh-Han, Steinhardt, NeurIPS 2024 — [arXiv:2402.18563](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563))**
A retrieval-augmented + structured-reasoning system that self-supervises by fine-tuning on the model's own outputs that beat the crowd. On a ~900-question judgemental-forecasting set it nears (but doesn't beat) the human aggregate — the first automated system to reach near-crowd level. Follow-on analysis notes base models are typically *overconfident* (especially on likely events) and that the fine-tuning was partly a calibration fix.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - Retrieval over a corpus of relevant prior cases (their news retrieval) is the direct analog of our DreamGym-style trace retrieval — grounding a prediction in similar past runs is a proven lever.
  - Self-supervised fine-tuning on "predictions that turned out right" is a cheap way to sharpen and calibrate an estimator without extra human labels.
  - Off-the-shelf LLMs are overconfident by default; expect to need an explicit calibration step for FF4's numbers to be trustworthy.

**Wisdom of the silicon crowd: LLM ensemble prediction rivals human crowd accuracy (Schoenegger et al., Science Advances 2024 — [arXiv:2402.19379](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.19379), [journal](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adp1528))**
Aggregating forecasts from an ensemble of 12 LLMs matches the accuracy of a human forecasting-tournament crowd, and exposing a single LLM to the human median forecast improves it. Shows that ensembling/aggregation recovers much of the "wisdom of crowds" effect in silico.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - Ensembling multiple model predictions (or multiple sampled WM rollouts) is a cheap variance-reduction/calibration trick we can apply to pre-run estimates.
  - Anchoring on any available human or heuristic prior improves the model — suggests FF4 should feed in cheap baseline estimates (e.g., analytical throughput bounds) as context, not predict from scratch.

**ForecastBench: A Dynamic Benchmark of AI Forecasting Capabilities (Karger et al., ICLR 2025 — [arXiv:2409.19839](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19839))**
A continuously-updated, contamination-free forecasting benchmark that pulls new unresolved questions daily from nine sources (prediction markets, forecasting platforms, real-world time series) and scores LLMs, human generalists, and superforecasters on a live leaderboard. Finding: LLMs trail expert forecasters — the human ceiling is not yet reached.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - The "questions must be unresolved at submission time" design is the gold standard for avoiding leakage; our WM eval on future/expensive runs should adopt the same discipline (predict before the run finishes, grade after).
  - The gap to expert humans is a caution: on genuinely open-ended forecasting, LLMs are good-not-great, so FF4 should target *narrow, well-instrumented* processes where trace grounding is strong rather than open judgemental forecasting.

**Predicting replication: DARPA SCORE, Replication Markets & "deep replicability" (2020 — [PNAS: Estimating deep replicability with human + AI](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1909046117), [Replication Markets](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.04543), [SCORE community forecasts, R.Soc.Open Sci. 2020](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsos.200566))**
DARPA's SCORE program generated confidence scores for ~3,000 social/behavioural science claims using prediction markets, surveys, and ML text models. Key result: an ML-only model built from paper text predicts replication about as well as prediction markets, and combining ML with markets/surveys beats any single method. Expected replication rates differ by field (econ ~58%, psych/education ~42%).
- Learnings for FF4:
  - Direct precedent that a model reading only the *description* of an experiment predicts its outcome as well as expensive human elicitation — validates the "predict from the plan, not the run" premise.
  - Best accuracy came from *combining* a cheap model signal with a more expensive human/market signal — argues FF4 should be positioned as a cheap first-pass filter feeding a human/real-run decision, not a full replacement.
  - Outcome-prediction accuracy is domain-dependent; per-domain calibration matters.

**Learning-curve extrapolation for early stopping (Domhan et al. 2015; Baker et al.; Chandrashekaran & Lane; recent GraphODE — [arXiv:2412.15554](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15554))**
A mature line predicting a network's *final* accuracy from the first few epochs of its learning curve, via parametric ensembles, affine transforms of historical curves, or SVM/graph models over architecture features. Used to terminate unpromising runs early, reported to speed model selection up to ~20x.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - This is the "early-signal" complement to pure pre-run prediction: if FF4 can watch the first slice of an OTel trace, curve-extrapolation techniques port directly to experiment-level outcome estimation (final metric, OOM risk, convergence).
  - These methods are cheap, well-understood, and give calibrated uncertainty — a strong baseline our WM should be measured against, not ignore.
  - Leveraging *historical* curves from similar runs (Chandrashekaran & Lane) is again the retrieval-over-traces pattern.

**Autonomous AI-scientist systems: Sakana AI Scientist v1/v2 (2024/2025 — [v1 arXiv:2408.06292](https://sakana.ai/ai-scientist/), [v2 arXiv:2504.08066](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.08066))**
End-to-end pipelines that generate ideas, run experiments, write and even review papers; v2 produced the first AI-generated workshop paper to pass peer review, at ~$6–15 per paper. Notably, idea *selection* leans on an LLM "reviewer"/novelty score to rank ideas before committing compute — an implicit pre-run outcome estimate — but there is no calibrated forecast of experimental success, and independent evals ([arXiv:2502.14297](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.14297)) report mixed real-world quality.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - These systems already *want* a pre-run estimator (they burn compute on ideas that fail) but currently use only a coarse LLM-review score — a well-calibrated FF4 outcome predictor is a concrete drop-in value-add.
  - Their cheap, high-volume runs could become a data source: many idea→experiment→result triples per dollar, i.e. affordable ground-truth for an outcome corpus.
  - Beware self-evaluation bias: an LLM that both proposes and judges its own experiments is optimistic — FF4 predictions should be graded against real outcomes, not model self-scores.

**Google AI co-scientist (DeepMind, Nature 2026 — [blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/co-scientist-a-multi-agent-ai-partner-to-accelerate-research/), [arXiv:2502.18864](https://collimateur.uqam.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2025/03/2502.18864v1.pdf))**
A Gemini-based multi-agent system (generation, reflection/critique, ranking, evolution agents) that proposes and refines research hypotheses, using an Elo-style "idea tournament" of simulated scientific debates to rank candidates before any wet-lab work. Reported to cut early hypothesis generation from weeks to days and produce proposals rated more novel by domain experts on biomedical goals.
- Learnings for FF4:
  - The Elo tournament is essentially *comparative* pre-run outcome estimation (which hypothesis will pan out?) — comparative ranking is often easier and better-calibrated than absolute prediction, worth considering for FF4's API.
  - It ranks *before* committing expensive experiments, exactly FF4's use case, but validation is on expert-rated novelty rather than measured experimental success — the "does the prediction match reality" loop is still open.

**LLMs as simulated subjects: Homo Silicus & Generative Agent Simulations (Horton 2023 — [SSRN 4413859](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4413859); Park et al. 2024 — [arXiv:2411.10109](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.10109))**
Horton's "homo silicus" shows GPT reproduces classic economics experiments (dictator/ultimatum games, status-quo bias, fairness) qualitatively, positioning LLMs as a way to *pilot experiments before costly human studies*. Park et al. build generative agents from 2-hour interviews with 1,052 real people; agents match participants' General Social Survey answers 85% as well as the people match themselves two weeks later, and replicate 4/5 social-science experiments the humans replicated. Related applied work (CXSimulator, AgentA/B) simulates users to pre-screen marketing A/B tests, though pure-LLM content-prediction can be near-random ([LOLA, Marketing Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2024.0990)).
- Learnings for FF4:
  - Strongest evidence that simulating the *process* (agents/subjects) rather than directly regressing the outcome can predict aggregate experimental results — analogous to the WM simulating a run instead of guessing its metric.
  - The 85%-of-self-consistency figure is a useful realism ceiling: even a good simulator won't beat the intrinsic noise of the real process, so FF4 should report predictions relative to run-to-run variance, not against a noiseless ideal.
  - A/B-simulation results are uneven (some near random) — reliability is task-specific and must be validated per domain before trusting the estimate.

### Takeaways for FF4

1. **Pre-run outcome prediction already works in narrow, well-described settings — but not open-endedly.** BrainBench, DARPA SCORE, and homo-silicus show models predicting experimental/replication outcomes from descriptions at expert level; ForecastBench and RE-Bench show open-ended forecasting/execution still trails top humans. FF4 should aim at narrow, heavily-instrumented processes (OOM, throughput, P&R) where trace grounding is strong, not general "will this research succeed" forecasting.

2. **Calibration is the whole game, and default LLMs are overconfident.** Halawi et al. and downstream analyses document systematic overconfidence; BrainBench and Halawi's fine-tuning show calibration is achievable but must be engineered (fine-tune on verified outcomes, ensemble, or abstain). FF4 must ship an explicit calibration step and report confidence-vs-accuracy, ideally with a selective-prediction/abstain path.

3. **Retrieval-over-prior-runs and ensembling are the proven levers.** Every strong system (Halawi's news retrieval, silicon-crowd ensembling, historical learning-curve transfer, SCORE's model+market combo) wins by grounding in similar past cases and aggregating — validating our DreamGym trace-retrieval + WM approach, and suggesting comparative/ranking outputs (co-scientist's Elo tournament) as an easier-to-calibrate alternative to absolute point estimates.

4. **Early-signal extrapolation is a cheap, strong baseline we shouldn't skip.** Learning-curve extrapolation predicts final metrics from the first few epochs with calibrated uncertainty and ~20x selection speedups. If FF4 can observe the first slice of a run's OTel trace, this is both a direct method and the baseline any WM predictor must beat.

5. **An MLE-bench/PaperBench-derived training corpus is expensive and better used as a held-out test set than as bulk training data.** Each ground-truth point is a multi-hour GPU agent run; neither benchmark ships a large ready-made corpus of run logs (you regenerate them via the harness). RE-Bench's released human score-vs-time trajectories and BrainBench's forced-choice items are the cheapest existing gold data. Practical path: use PaperBench/MLE-bench/RE-Bench as *evaluation* targets, and grow the training corpus from OTel traces of runs we're doing anyway (plus cheap high-volume AI-scientist-style runs) rather than a dedicated collection campaign.

6. **Don't let the predictor grade itself.** AI-scientist and co-scientist systems currently rank ideas with LLM self-review or novelty ratings, which are optimistic and not validated against measured outcomes. FF4's credibility depends on closing the loop against *real* results (like MLE-bench leaderboards, RE-Bench scores, SCORE replications), with contamination controls (ForecastBench-style "unresolved at prediction time") baked into the eval.
## Superoptimization & code-optimization search: predicting before measuring

The through-line across this literature: every superoptimizer and code-search system has an *expensive verification-or-measurement step* (SMT equivalence proof, cycle-accurate simulation, or on-hardware benchmarking) that dominates cost, and the systems that scale are the ones that interpose a *cheap predictor* — a test-case proxy, a learned classifier, a value model, or a full world model — to decide which candidates deserve the expensive step. That is exactly FF4's thesis, and this domain has ~40 years of evidence for it.

**Superoptimizer: A Look at the Smallest Program (Massalin, ASPLOS 1987, [PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs343/resources/superoptimizer.pdf))**
The paper that coined "superoptimization": given an instruction set, exhaustively search for the shortest instruction sequence computing a target function. Massalin's key trick was a *probabilistic test* — check candidate equivalence against a handful of test vectors first, and only escalate to full verification for the rare survivors — which is what made exhaustive search tractable at all.
Learnings for FF4:
- The founding move of the field is *cheap-proxy-before-expensive-check*: a fast probabilistic screen gates the slow correctness proof. FF4's estimator is the modern generalization of Massalin's test-vector filter.
- Establishes the baseline economics: brute-force search over a candidate space is only viable if the per-candidate cost is collapsed for the overwhelming majority that will fail.

**Automatic Generation of Peephole Superoptimizers (Bansal & Aiken, ASPLOS 2006, [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1168857.1168906))**
Rather than search at compile time, this system runs the expensive enumerate-and-verify superoptimization *once, offline*, harvesting verified equivalent instruction-sequence pairs into a lookup table that the compiler then applies in near-constant time. It amortizes an enormous one-time search cost into a cheap runtime table lookup.
Learnings for FF4:
- The purest form of "predict before measuring": pre-compute the answer table so the expensive step never runs in the hot path. FF4's world model is analogous — a learned artifact that replaces re-running the real evaluation.
- Value quantification: converts a per-invocation search (seconds–hours) into an O(1) table hit; the entire ROI is the amortization ratio (times the table is reused).

**Stochastic Superoptimization / STOKE (Schkufza, Sharma & Aiken, ASPLOS 2013, [PDF](https://theory.stanford.edu/~aiken/publications/papers/asplos13.pdf); [arXiv 1211.0557](https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.0557); [code](https://github.com/StanfordPL/stoke))**
STOKE frames loop-free x86-64 superoptimization as MCMC search over a cost function that fuses a *correctness term* (Hamming distance = number of correct output-register bits over a test-case set) with a *performance term*. The cheap test-case cost drives millions of proposals; full SMT-based equivalence verification is reserved only for candidates that already look correct and fast. Starting from `-O0` binaries it matches or beats `gcc -O3` and sometimes expert hand-written assembly.
Learnings for FF4:
- Canonical two-tier architecture: a *fast approximate scorer* (test cases) explored en masse, a *slow ground-truth verifier* (SMT) invoked sparingly. FF4's estimator occupies the fast-scorer tier.
- The performance term is itself a *learned/modeled cost proxy* rather than real timing — STOKE already treats "predict the measured outcome" as a first-class design choice, not just correctness.
- Value: the SMT verification is the throughput bottleneck; because the test-case cost gates it, the search explores orders of magnitude more candidates than a verify-everything loop could.

**Souper: A Synthesizing Superoptimizer (Sasnauskas et al., arXiv 2017, [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.04422); [code](https://github.com/google/souper))**
Souper synthesizes missing peephole optimizations for LLVM IR via a CEGIS loop backed by an SMT solver (Z3/CVC4/STP/Boolector), enumerating symbolic candidates like `x + C` and solving for constants. It found real optimizations that shrank the Clang-3.9 binary by 4.4%. The SMT equivalence check is explicitly the pipeline's dominant cost, which motivated the pruning work below.
Learnings for FF4:
- A concrete, still-maintained testbed where "the expensive step" is unambiguously the solver call — an ideal shape for a pre-run endorse/reject estimator.
- Correctness (not just speed) is the bottleneck here; FF4's "predict failure states" output maps directly onto "predict this candidate won't verify."

**Dataflow-based Pruning for Speeding up Superoptimization (Regehr group, University of Utah, [PDF](https://www-old.cs.utah.edu/~regehr/dataflow-pruning.pdf))** *(author list/venue not cleanly verifiable — PDF parse failed; kept because it is PrediPrune's published baseline)*
Uses cheap dataflow analyses to prune candidate optimizations that cannot possibly be correct *before* the SMT solver is invoked, cutting synthesis time in Souper. It is the hand-engineered predecessor to the learned pruner below and the baseline PrediPrune measures against.
Learnings for FF4:
- Demonstrates that even a *non-learned* static predictor of "will this survive verification?" pays for itself — a lower bound on what a learned estimator should beat.
- Frames pruning as a precision/recall tradeoff: prune too aggressively and you discard real optimizations; this is exactly FF4's false-negative (missed good candidate) vs. wasted-eval tradeoff.

**PrediPrune: Reducing Verification Overhead in Souper with ML (Ishimwe, Shivakumar, Kim, Lehman, Izraelevitz; arXiv 2025, [abs 2509.16497](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16497))**
An MLP classifier extracts features from each candidate optimization and predicts whether the SMT verification will succeed, pruning likely-failures before the solver runs. It reduces Souper's compilation/verification time by **51% vs. the baseline and by a further 12% vs. dataflow-only pruning**, and exposes a knob to trade compile time against optimizations retained.
Learnings for FF4:
- The closest analogue in this domain to FF4: a *learned* pre-run predictor of the expensive outcome, beating a strong hand-engineered predictor by 12% and the naive baseline by 51%.
- Value quantification: ~2x reduction in the verification-dominated pipeline purely from a lightweight learned "will it pass?" classifier — and it ships a tunable precision knob, which FF4 should mirror (endorsement threshold vs. recall of true wins).

**MILEPOST GCC: Machine Learning Based Research Compiler (Fursin et al., 2008/2011, [PDF](https://inria.hal.science/inria-00294704/PDF/fmtp2008.pdf); [journal](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10766-010-0161-2))**
The first widely-cited ML autotuning compiler: a 1-nearest-neighbor model maps static program features to a predicted-good set of optimization flags, learned from a repository (cTuning) of program-features → measured-behavior records. It cut MiBench execution time ~11% on an ARC processor without per-program search.
Learnings for FF4:
- Establishes the "features → predicted best configuration, skip the search" pattern that FF4 generalizes with an LLM. Even a 1-NN over a trace corpus beats running the sweep.
- The value is search elimination: instead of benchmarking many flag combinations per program, predict one good config from a corpus of prior measurements — directly analogous to FF4 estimating from a trace corpus.

**CompilerGym (Cummins et al., CGO 2022, distinguished paper, [code](https://github.com/facebookresearch/CompilerGym); [Meta blog](https://ai.meta.com/blog/compilergym-making-compiler-optimizations-accessible-to-all/))**
Packages compiler-optimization tasks (LLVM phase ordering, etc.) as Gym RL environments with search spaces up to ~10^4461 (vastly larger than Go). It standardizes the observation/reward interface so learned agents can be trained and compared, and is the substrate most learned-cost-model compiler work now builds on.
Learnings for FF4:
- Quantifies *why* prediction matters: the candidate space is astronomically large, so measuring even a tiny fraction is infeasible — the estimator must do nearly all the filtering.
- A ready-made harness for the "propose → predict → selectively measure" loop; FF4 could benchmark its estimator against CompilerGym's real reward signal.

**CompilerDream: Learning a Compiler World Model for General Code Optimization (Deng, Wu, Feng, Wang, Long; 2024, [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16077v2))**
A model-based RL agent that learns an explicit *world model* of LLVM: representation + transition + reward decoders that predict the IR state and instruction-count reward of applying an optimization pass, letting the agent roll out thousands of imagined optimization trajectories *without invoking the real compiler*. Trained on 110,240 CodeContests programs, it tops the CompilerGym leaderboard (1.073x code-size reduction on cBench, guided search) using less wall-clock than competitors and beats `-Oz` zero-shot on unseen programs.
Learnings for FF4:
- The most literal precedent for FF4: a *world model that predicts the measured outcome (reward) of a proposed action before running the real system*, used to make search cheap. Validates the entire framing on a code-optimization domain.
- Value: "simulate thousands of trajectories without the real compiler" is the compute-multiplier claim; the win is fewer real compiler invocations per unit of search progress plus lower wall-clock than model-free RL.

**Meta Large Language Model Compiler (Cummins et al., 2024, [arXiv 2407.02524](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.02524))**
A Code-Llama-derived 7B/13B model pretrained on 546B tokens of LLVM-IR and assembly, instruction-tuned to emulate compiler behavior and predict good optimization decisions. It reaches **77% of the code-size improvement of a full autotuning search** and does 45% disassembly round-trips (14% exact match) — i.e., captures most of the search's value in a single forward pass.
Learnings for FF4:
- Direct evidence that an LLM can *internalize the outcome of an expensive search* and replay most of it cheaply: 77% of autotuning's benefit with no search loop.
- Suggests FF4's estimator can be a fine-tuned code/IR model, and gives a concrete "% of oracle search value recovered" metric to target.

**Learning Performance-Improving Code Edits / PIE (Shypula et al., ICLR 2024 spotlight, [site](https://pie4perf.com/); [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=ix7rLVHXyY); [code](https://github.com/LearningOpt/pie))**
77K+ human C++ optimization pairs with unit tests; models learn to emit performance-improving edits. Crucially, performance is measured with the **gem5 Skylake simulator, not wall-clock, because reproducible benchmarking on commodity hardware is "very hard"** — the measurement method is itself a determinism-vs-realism design choice. Best model (self-play fine-tuned GPT-3.5, best-of-40) hits 9.64x aggregate speedup / 99.8% optimization rate; retrieval-prompted GPT-4 gets 2.53x / 51% at best@1.
Learnings for FF4:
- The gem5 decision is a direct FF4 lesson: when real measurement is noisy/non-reproducible, teams already reach for a *deterministic surrogate*. FF4's estimator is the learned end of that same spectrum.
- Value quantification of the measurement problem: the reason best-of-40 beats best@1 (9.64x vs ~2.5x) is that many candidates must be evaluated — precisely the multiplier a good pre-run predictor would let you avoid paying in full.

**Supersonic: Learning to Generate Source Code Optimizations in C/C++ (Chen, Fang & Monperrus; IEEE TSE 2024, [arXiv 2309.14846](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14846))**
A seq2seq model more than 600x smaller than GPT-3.5 (and ~3700x smaller than GPT-4) trained specifically on C/C++ optimizing edits, which nonetheless out-optimizes both frontier models on the task.
Learnings for FF4:
- A clean "small specialized predictor beats giant general model" data point — capacity for the *task*, not general capability, is what matters; echoes the embeddings-for-regression finding that bigger models don't automatically regress better.
- Supports keeping FF4's serving cost low: a modest trace-specialized model may beat a frontier model prompted generically.

**LLM Program Optimization via Retrieval-Augmented Search / RAS (Anupam, Shypula, Bastani; 2025, [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.18916); [project](https://sagnikanupam.com/papers/llmprogramoptimization))**
Retrieves semantically similar prior optimizations to guide a targeted search over program edits rather than sampling blindly. It performs **up to 2.06x better than prior state-of-the-art black-box adaptation** on C++ optimization at matched budget, i.e., a retrieval prior makes each evaluated candidate more likely to be a real improvement.
Learnings for FF4:
- Retrieval over a corpus of past outcomes sharpens the candidate distribution *before* evaluation — the same corpus-conditioning FF4 uses. Quantified as a 2.06x efficiency edge from better candidate selection.
- Confirms that pairing retrieval (memory of prior measured results) with search is the winning recipe, echoing FF4's trace-corpus grounding.

### Takeaways for FF4

- **Every mature system in this domain is two-tiered: a cheap predictor gates an expensive verifier/benchmark.** This has been the load-bearing pattern since Massalin (1987, test-vector screen) through STOKE (test-case Hamming distance before SMT) to PrediPrune (learned MLP before SMT). FF4 is not a novel gamble here — it is the current frontier of a 40-year-old, repeatedly-validated idea.
- **The expensive step is either an SMT equivalence proof or a hardware/simulator benchmark, and it is the throughput bottleneck.** Souper and STOKE are solver-bound; KernelBench and PIE are measurement-bound (and PIE resorts to gem5 because real timing isn't reproducible). Naming *which* expensive step you're replacing determines what the estimator must predict (correctness/failure vs. speedup number) — FF4 does both.
- **Value quantification, per subdomain:** learned pre-run pruning cuts solver-bound superoptimization time ~2x (PrediPrune: 51% vs baseline, +12% over hand-engineered dataflow pruning); a world model recovers most of an autotuning search's value while cutting real-compiler calls (CompilerDream tops CompilerGym at lower wall-clock; Meta LLM Compiler = 77% of full-autotuning benefit in one pass); retrieval-guided candidate selection gives ~2.06x search efficiency (RAS); and offline precomputation (Bansal-Aiken) drives per-invocation cost to O(1). The consistent headline is **roughly 2x fewer expensive evaluations to ~most-of-oracle value.**
- **Search spaces are astronomically large (CompilerGym: ~10^4461), so measuring even a tiny fraction is infeasible — the estimator must carry almost the entire filtering load.** The multiplier from prediction grows with the space; FF4 should report value as "fraction of oracle search value recovered per real evaluation spent," the metric Meta LLM Compiler (77%) and CompilerDream implicitly use.
- **A learned predictor beats a strong hand-engineered one, but both beat brute force.** PrediPrune's +12% over dataflow pruning is the direct evidence that FF4's learned estimator should be benchmarked against, not just against no-prediction — and it should ship a tunable endorsement threshold (PrediPrune's compile-time-vs-opportunity knob) exposing the false-negative/wasted-eval tradeoff explicitly.
- **Reproducible-measurement pain is a first-class motivation, not a footnote.** PIE's switch to gem5 shows practitioners already replace noisy real measurement with deterministic surrogates; FF4's estimator is the learned extreme of that same move, and should be positioned as "cheaper *and* more reproducible than the real eval," with the caveat (from KernelBench's <20% correctness and 21.4x cross-hardware speedup variance) that the estimator inherits whatever variance the real environment has.
## Databases: cardinality estimation, query cost prediction & what-if analysis

The database community has been building and shipping *pre-run estimators* — models that predict a query's cost or an index's benefit *before* paying to execute it — for more than 25 years. This is the closest existing analog to FF4's premise, and it comes with a hard-won literature on where such estimators fail, why accuracy metrics mislead, and how much money accurate prediction is actually worth.

**How Good Are Query Optimizers, Really? (Leis et al., PVLDB Vol. 9 / VLDB 2015, [pdf](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol9/p204-leis.pdf))**
The canonical evidence that misestimation, not cost modeling or plan search, is what breaks query optimizers. Using their newly-introduced Join Order Benchmark (JOB — 33 realistic multi-join queries over IMDb, 3–16 joins, ~8 on average, released with the paper), they show industrial cardinality estimators routinely err by *many orders of magnitude*, that errors are dominated by *underestimation that compounds as joins are added*, and that the cost model contributes at most ~30% error for a given cardinality while the cardinality model "can quite easily introduce errors of many orders of magnitude." Roughly 10% of JOB queries failed to complete in reasonable time on PostgreSQL 9.4 purely because of estimation errors. A 2018 journal extension ("Query Optimization Through the Looking Glass," [pdf](https://db.in.tum.de/~leis/papers/lookingglass.pdf)) and a 2025 restudy ("Still Asking…", [doi](https://doi.org/10.14778/3750601.3760521)) confirm the finding still holds.
- **Learnings for FF4:** The prediction *is* the product — plan quality is bottlenecked almost entirely by estimation accuracy, not by the machinery that consumes the estimate. Quantified value: fixing estimates turns "never finishes" queries into completing ones and removes order-of-magnitude slowdowns.
- Error compounds with structural depth (joins). FF4 should expect its own error to grow with configuration complexity/length and measure it as a function of that, not as a single aggregate.
- Underestimation is systematically more dangerous than overestimation. A pre-run estimator's *error asymmetry* matters more than its mean error.

**Learned Cardinalities: Estimating Correlated Joins with Deep Learning / MSCN (Kipf et al., CIDR 2019, [arXiv:1809.00677](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.00677), [code](https://github.com/andreaskipf/learnedcardinalities))**
Introduced the multi-set convolutional network (MSCN), a *query-driven* supervised model that featurizes a query as sets of tables/joins/predicates and regresses the true cardinality, learning join-crossing correlations that sampling misses. Trained on query→true-cardinality pairs; ships with the JOB-light workload.
- **Learnings for FF4:** This is the purest precedent for FF4's design — supervised (input configuration → measured outcome) trained on logged real executions. The featurization lesson (decompose the structured input into typed sets rather than one flat vector) transfers directly.
- Query-driven models are only as good as their training workload's coverage; they degrade on query shapes unseen in training. FF4 must track train/serve distribution shift explicitly.

**Deep Unsupervised Cardinality Estimation / Naru (Yang et al., PVLDB Vol. 13 / VLDB 2020, [arXiv:1905.04278](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.04278), [code](https://github.com/naru-project/naru))**
A *data-driven* approach: learn the joint distribution of a table with a deep autoregressive model (MADE/ResMADE/Transformer) and answer range queries via a Monte-Carlo integration scheme ("progressive sampling"). Reports up to **90× accuracy improvement** over prior estimators, needing no query training set.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Modeling the underlying data/world distribution (data-driven) rather than the query→answer map (query-driven) is the alternative axis. It generalizes to arbitrary queries but requires an inference-time integration trick — a reminder that a good world model may still need a nontrivial decoder to answer a specific question.

**NeuroCard: One Cardinality Estimator for All Tables (Yang et al., PVLDB Vol. 14 No. 1 / VLDB 2021, [arXiv:2006.08109](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08109), [code](https://github.com/neurocard/neurocard))**
Extends Naru to *whole-schema* joins with a single density model, no inter-table/inter-column independence assumptions. On JOB-light it reaches a max Q-error of **8.5× using only 4 MB (a 4.6× improvement over prior SOTA)**; on the harder 16-table JOB-M it is **~10× more accurate** at 27 MB.
- **Learnings for FF4:** One general model over the whole domain can beat a patchwork of per-slice models, and small models can carry big accuracy gains — relevant if FF4 wants a single estimator across configuration families rather than many narrow ones. Note the metric is *worst-case* Q-error, not mean: they optimize the tail, because the tail is what kills plans.

**DeepDB: Learn from Data, not from Queries! (Hilprecht et al., PVLDB Vol. 13 / VLDB 2020, [pdf](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol13/p992-hilprecht.pdf), [code](https://github.com/DataManagementLab/deepdb-public))**
Uses Relational Sum-Product Networks to capture the joint distribution across a schema, serving cardinality estimation *and* approximate query answering from one model without workload-specific training. Emphasizes fast inference and incremental updates.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Explicitly designed around the operational constraints that sink learned estimators — cheap inference and *incremental maintainability* as data changes. FF4 should treat "how fast can the model be refreshed when the world moves" as a first-class design axis, not an afterthought.

**Are We Ready For Learned Cardinality Estimation? (Wang et al., PVLDB Vol. 14 No. 9 / VLDB 2021 Best Paper, [arXiv:2012.06743](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.06743), [code](https://github.com/sfu-db/AreCELearnedYet))**
A rigorous production-readiness audit of single-table learned estimators. Confirms learned models *are* more accurate than traditional histograms/sampling, but they "suffer from high training and inference costs," "cannot catch up with fast data updates and return large errors," and their behavior is "much harder to interpret and often unpredictable" under shifts in correlation, skew, or domain size.
- **Learnings for FF4:** The single most transferable paper here. Accuracy in the lab is necessary but nowhere near sufficient; the blockers to deployment are *update lag under drift, cost, instability, and interpretability*. FF4 needs a drift-detection + refresh story and a trust/uncertainty story, or it will lose to a cheap baseline in production despite winning on accuracy.

**Cardinality Estimation in DBMS: A Comprehensive Benchmark Evaluation (Han et al., PVLDB Vol. 15 No. 4 / VLDB 2022, [arXiv:2109.05877](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05877))**
Plugs learned estimators into PostgreSQL and measures *end-to-end* plan quality, not just estimator accuracy. Key finding: better Q-error does **not** reliably yield better query plans, because Q-error weights all sub-plan estimates equally when only a few matter. Proposes P-Error, a metric that correlates with actual end-to-end runtime, and releases the STATS-CEB benchmark.
- **Learnings for FF4:** A direct warning about metric design. FF4 must validate its estimator on the *downstream decision it enables* (did we pick the right configuration / avoid the expensive run?), not on raw prediction error — and should define its evaluation metric to weight the predictions that actually change decisions.

**Plan-Structured Deep Neural Network Models for Query Performance Prediction (Marcus & Papaemmanouil, PVLDB Vol. 12 / VLDB 2019, [arXiv:1902.00132](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.00132))**
Predicts a query's *latency* before running it by assembling a neural network isomorphic to the query plan tree (one learned model per operator), eliminating hand-crafted features and beating prior human-engineered QPP models.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Latency-before-execution is exactly FF4's "predict the measured number." Structuring the predictor to mirror the structure of the input (here, the plan tree) is a strong inductive-bias lesson. Value: accurate latency prediction is what enables admission control, scheduling, and SLA enforcement without speculative execution.

**Bao: Making Learned Query Optimization Practical (Marcus et al., SIGMOD 2021 Best Paper, [doi](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3448016.3452838), [arXiv:2004.03814](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03814))**
Rather than replacing the optimizer, Bao *steers* it with per-query hints chosen by a tree-CNN + Thompson-sampling bandit, learning online from observed executions. On IMDb it cut cloud cost **>50% ($4.60 → $2.20)** and wall-clock **from ~6 hours to ~3**, dropped **99th-percentile latency from 130 s to under 20 s (~6.5×)**, and reached parity with PostgreSQL from a cold start within ~2 hours (vs 24h+ for earlier learned optimizers).
- **Learnings for FF4:** The blueprint for *practical* deployment: keep the trusted baseline in the loop, let the learned component nudge rather than replace, learn continuously from real outcomes, and explicitly optimize tail behavior and cold-start time. Quantified value of a good pre-run predictor: ~50% cost and ~6.5× tail-latency reduction. This "advise, don't override" pattern is likely FF4's safest production posture.

**AutoAdmin "What-If" Index Analysis Utility (Chaudhuri & Narasayya, SIGMOD 1998, [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/autoadmin-what-if-index-analysis-utility/))**
The original shipped pre-run estimator: DBAs propose *hypothetical* indexes and the optimizer estimates their impact on workload cost **without ever building them**. This "what-if" API became the foundation of index advisors in SQL Server (and later HypoPG for PostgreSQL) and has run in production DBMSs for ~25 years.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Proof that "predict the outcome of a candidate configuration before paying to build it" is a durable, commercially successful product category. The interface lesson: expose it as a *what-if API* over candidate configurations, reusing the same cost model the real system uses so predictions stay consistent with reality.

**AI Meets AI: Leveraging Query Executions to Improve Index Recommendations (Ding et al., SIGMOD 2019, [pdf](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2019/04/regression_sigmod2019_CR.pdf))**
Documents the failure mode of the 1998 what-if estimator: because it relies on optimizer cost estimates, in a *significant fraction* of cases an index it predicts will help actually makes the query *slower* once built — a major blocker for autonomous indexing. Reframing "is plan A cheaper than plan B?" as an ML classification task over execution features gives up to **5× fewer pairwise-comparison errors and eliminates almost all execution-cost regressions** when embedded in an index tuner.
- **Learnings for FF4:** The regression problem is the real enemy of a deployed estimator — a change predicted to help that hurts erodes trust faster than any accuracy metric captures. Predicting *relative* outcomes ("will this candidate beat the current one?") can be framed as classification and made far more robust than predicting absolute numbers. FF4 should measure and minimize regressions specifically, not just average error.

**DB-BERT (SIGMOD 2022, [doi](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3514221.3517843)) and GPTuner (Lao et al., VLDB 2024, [arXiv:2311.03157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03157))** — LLM-based DB tuning
DB-BERT fine-tunes BERT to "read the manual," turning natural-language tuning hints into knob settings refined by RL. GPTuner uses GPT-4 to distill manuals/forums into a structured "Tuning Lake" that constrains a Bayesian-optimization search, reporting **up to 30% better throughput/latency and ~16× faster convergence** than prior methods including DB-BERT.
- **Learnings for FF4:** The current frontier of *LLMs as priors for systems optimization* — the LLM supplies world knowledge that shrinks the search space, while a cheap empirical loop verifies against measured outcomes. Directly validates FF4's thesis that an LLM's prior about a configuration's outcome is valuable *if paired with real-measurement grounding*. The 16× convergence speedup is the quantified payoff of a good prior over blind search.

### Takeaways for FF4

1. **Misestimation is the dominant cost, and the field proved it with numbers.** Leis et al. established that estimation error — not the surrounding machinery — is what produces order-of-magnitude slowdowns and non-completing queries. A pre-run estimator that is accurate where it matters is genuinely the highest-leverage component; FF4 is targeting the right problem.
2. **Accuracy metrics lie about deployment value.** Han et al. showed lower Q-error does not mean better plans; you must evaluate on the *downstream decision the estimate drives* and weight the predictions that actually change outcomes. FF4 should define its metric around "did the prediction change the right decision / avoid the expensive run," and measure error asymmetry (underestimation is worse) and the *tail*, as NeuroCard/Bao do.
3. **Regressions destroy trust faster than error improves it.** The 1998→2019 arc (AutoAdmin → "AI Meets AI") is a 20-year lesson that a single confidently-wrong prediction that makes things worse is what gets an estimator switched off. Track and minimize regression rate explicitly; consider predicting *relative* comparisons (classification) for robustness; keep a trusted baseline in the loop and *advise rather than override* (Bao).
4. **Robustness under drift, cheap inference, and fast refresh beat peak accuracy.** "Are We Ready?" (Wang et al.) found learned estimators lose to simple baselines in production because they can't keep up with data updates, cost too much to run, and behave unpredictably. FF4 needs a drift-detection + incremental-refresh + uncertainty/interpretability story or it won't survive contact with production.
5. **LLM-as-prior + empirical verification is a validated, quantified pattern.** GPTuner's ~16× faster convergence and ~30% performance gains show an LLM's world knowledge is most valuable when it *constrains* the search space and is then checked against real measurements — precisely FF4's proposed loop.
6. **Value-quantification summary (what accurate pre-run prediction is worth, with sources):** removing order-of-magnitude plan slowdowns and turning non-completing queries into completing ones (Leis 2015); up to **90×** (Naru) and **4.6–10×** (NeuroCard) accuracy gains that translate to better plans; **>50% cloud cost and ~6.5× tail-latency** reduction end-to-end (Bao); **~5× fewer plan-comparison errors, near-zero regressions** in index tuning (Ding 2019); **~30% performance / 16× faster tuning** for LLM-guided knob search (GPTuner). Released assets to reuse: the JOB benchmark (Leis), MSCN/Naru/NeuroCard/DeepDB code, AreCELearnedYet (Wang), STATS-CEB (Han), and the MIT [Cardinality Estimation Benchmark](https://learnedsystems.mit.edu/cardinality-estimation-benchmark/).
## The broader map: what else is worth predicting before you run it

The unifying pattern across our v1 domains (ML memory/OOM, vLLM throughput, FPGA P&R) is *pay-once-to-forecast, save-many-on-avoided-runs*. Below are 13 additional pre-run prediction targets where advance estimation is quantifiably valuable, verified against primary sources. Numbers are attributed; where a widely-quoted figure lacks a solid source, it is flagged.

**1. LLM routing / cascade — call the cheap model only when it suffices (FrugalGPT, arXiv 2305.05176, 2023; RouteLLM, ICLR 2025 / lm-sys, [repo](https://github.com/lm-sys/routellm), [FrugalGPT paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.05176))**
FrugalGPT learns an LLM cascade + scorer that decides, per query, whether a cheap model's answer is trustworthy before escalating to GPT-4. RouteLLM trains a binary router (strong vs. weak model) and publishes reproducible routers. Both are literally pre-run estimators of "will the cheap path be good enough?"
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** FrugalGPT reports up to **98% inference-cost reduction while matching GPT-4** — but this is on the *HEADLINES* financial-news dataset specifically, not a universal number. RouteLLM reports **~85% cost reduction at 95% of GPT-4 quality on MT-Bench** and ~2x cost reduction elsewhere. Third-party enterprise reports cluster at a more sober **40–70%**.
- **Learnings for FF4:** The valuable output is *calibrated confidence that a cheaper config meets a quality bar*, not just a point estimate — exactly our "predict outcome + failure state" framing. Headline savings are dataset-dependent; a corpus must span query distributions or the WM overfits to one benchmark's easy tail.

**2. Neural scaling laws — forecast a training run's final loss before spending the compute (Kaplan et al. 2020, arXiv 2001.08361; Hoffmann/Chinchilla 2022, arXiv 2203.15556; [analysis](https://mbrenndoerfer.com/writing/scaling-laws-neural-language-models-power-law-predictions))**
Fit L(N,D)=E+A/N^α+B/D^β on many small cheap runs, then extrapolate the loss of a frontier run before committing. This is the purest pre-run estimator in ML: the whole point is to avoid training many large models to find the best config.
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** Frontier pretraining runs cost tens of millions of dollars each; scaling laws let labs pick N/D from sub-1% cheap runs. Chinchilla itself showed the *previously compute-optimal* recipe was wrong, implying prior large runs were badly mis-provisioned — the cost of *not* predicting well is an entire wasted frontier run.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Strongest possible template for a WM — structured config (params, tokens, compute) → measured scalar (loss). Teaches that the corpus should include *small-but-informative* configs whose extrapolation is trusted, and that the interesting failures (loss spikes, divergence) are exactly the off-power-law events a WM must flag.

**3. CI predictive test selection & flaky-test prediction (Machalica et al., "Predictive Test Selection," ICSE-SEIP 2019, arXiv 1810.05286; Google TAP, [research.google](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/45880.pdf))**
Meta's gradient-boosted model predicts which tests a diff will break and runs only those. Google's Test Automation Platform and Transition-Prediction do the analogous selection at build scale. Flaky-test prediction forecasts nondeterministic failures so they aren't mistaken for real regressions.
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** Meta catches **>99.9% of regressions while running ~1/3 of impacted tests, ~2x infra efficiency**. Google's TAP runs **~150M test executions/day / >4 billion test cases/day** across 50k+ changes — a colossal compute bill where even single-digit-% selection savings are enormous. **99% of Google test executions pass**, i.e. almost all that compute is spent confirming green.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Massive, cheap, self-labeling corpora (every CI run is a labeled example). The prized skill is high-recall on rare failures under heavy class imbalance — the same problem as predicting rare OOM in our v1 domain.

**4. Deep-learning training-job failure prediction — DIRECTLY quantifies our v1 domain (Zhang et al., "An Empirical Study on Program Failures of Deep Learning Jobs," ICSE 2020, Distinguished Paper, [PDF](https://hongyujohn.github.io/icse20-main-199.pdf); Philly traces, ATC'19, [repo](https://github.com/msr-fiddle/philly-traces))**
4,960 real failures from Microsoft's Philly cluster, hand-classified into 20 categories. **48.0%** of failures occur in platform/environment interaction, **13.5%** are DL-specific. Crucially for us: **GPU out-of-memory is the single largest runtime-exception category (~65% within that dimension), and ~8.8% of ALL failures are GPU-memory exhaustion** — the largest DL-specific cause.
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** The paper states failures "especially after long execution times" cause "expensive waste of shared resources such as GPU, storage, and network I/O." Philly-lineage reliability studies (e.g., Meta's, arXiv 2410.21680) show large jobs failing on the order of hours-between-failures, each restart re-burning GPU-days. This is the concrete, published basis for why our OOM domain matters.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Cite this as the value anchor for domain #1. It also validates the "predict a failure *state*, not just a number" design — the taxonomy of 20 categories is a ready label space, and OOM is empirically the highest-value single state to predict.

**5. Cloud spot-instance eviction / interruption prediction (AWS EC2 Spot, [aws.amazon.com/ec2/spot](https://aws.amazon.com/ec2/spot/); third-party interruption forecasting, e.g. Sedai/nOps)**
Spot capacity is up to ~90% cheaper than on-demand but AWS reclaims it with only a 2-minute warning. Vendors train models on historical capacity signals to predict interruptions **up to ~60 minutes ahead** and pre-migrate workloads.
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** The saving is the **up to 90% discount you can only safely capture if you predict eviction** far enough ahead to checkpoint/drain; a bad prediction means lost work + on-demand fallback. Note: the "60-minute" horizon is a vendor claim, not peer-reviewed — treat as marketing-grade.
- **Learnings for FF4:** A time-to-event prediction with an asymmetric cost (missing an eviction >> false alarm). Good reminder that FF4 outputs should carry a *lead-time* and that the loss function must be cost-asymmetric, not symmetric RMSE.

**6. HPC queue-wait & job-runtime prediction (Springer J. Supercomputing 2025, [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11227-025-08221-7); "Predicting batch queue job wait times," arXiv 2204.13543)**
Predict when a submitted job will start and how long it will run, to enable backfill scheduling and informed urgent-workload placement. XGBoost on production traces (e.g. ARCHER2) predicts start time within 60 min **85%** of the time, within 6h **95%**.
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** Users chronically over-estimate walltime, which cripples backfill and wastes node-hours; better runtime prediction directly raises utilization on machines costing tens of millions. Value = idle-node-hours recovered on a scarce shared resource.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Two coupled targets (wait AND runtime) from one config — supports multi-output WMs. Self-labeling traces again; the hard part is the long tail of very-long jobs, mirroring our throughput-tail problem in vLLM.

**7. Network configuration verification — predict outages before deployment (Batfish, [batfish.org](https://batfish.org/), open-sourced by Intentionet/AWS; "Stopping Network Outages Before They Start")**
Batfish builds a behavioral model purely from device configs and predicts how the network forwards packets and reacts to failures, so proposed changes are validated offline in CI before they touch production.
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** Most large network incidents stem from buggy config changes; catching them pre-deploy is "orders-of-magnitude cheaper." Anchor the cost with **ITIC 2023/2024: >90% of large enterprises put a single hour of downtime above $300k, ~44% above $1M, and top verticals (banking, healthcare, etc.) above $5M/hour** ([ITIC](https://itic-corp.com/itic-2024-hourly-cost-of-downtime-report/)).
- **Learnings for FF4:** This is symbolic/simulation-based, not learned — a useful contrast. It shows the market will pay for *guaranteed* pre-run answers; an LLM WM competes on breadth/heterogeneous configs where a formal model is too costly to build, but must earn trust where formal tools already give certainty.

**8. Learned query optimizers / cardinality & cost estimation (Yang thesis, UC Berkeley EECS-2022-194, [PDF](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-194.pdf); Lero, arXiv 2302.06873; survey arXiv 2101.01507)**
Before executing a SQL plan, the optimizer estimates each sub-query's cardinality and the plan's cost (a proxy for latency) to pick the cheapest of thousands of candidate plans. Learned estimators (MSCN, Tree-LSTM/Transformer cost models) replace error-prone histogram heuristics.
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** Cardinality errors compound multiplicatively across joins and routinely cause plans that are orders of magnitude slower; the entire optimizer exists to avoid running bad plans. Value is per-query latency/compute avoided, at the scale of every analytical query in a warehouse.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Decades-old proof that "estimate cost from a structured spec, then choose" is a viable product surface. Reinforces that estimation error near decision boundaries (which plan wins) matters more than average error — FF4 should be evaluated on *decision regret*, not just calibration.

**9. Semiconductor tape-out / mask & respin cost — the ultimate expensive verification (IBS via Handel Jones, reported in [SemiEngineering "Big Trouble At 3nm"](https://semiengineering.com/big-trouble-at-3nm/) & [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/news/firm-estimates-a-2nm-chip-now-costs-dollar725-million-to-design))**
A single advanced-node tape-out commits an EUV mask set and a fab run that cannot be patched. IBS estimates full IC *design* cost at **~$542M at 5nm, $500M–$1.5B at 3nm, ~$725M at 2nm**; mask sets alone run **~$10–20M+ at 3nm**. A respin adds roughly **50–100% of initial mask cost** plus months of slip.
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** Every bug caught in pre-silicon verification (vs. a respin) avoids ~$10M+ and a multi-month schedule hit; a functional respin can sink a product's market window entirely. Caveat: SemiAnalysis argues IBS figures run **50–66% high**, so quote them as upper-bound industry estimates, not ground truth.
- **Learnings for FF4:** The extreme end of the value curve — where the real run is so expensive that even a mediocre WM pays off. Best candidate for "prediction value per query is astronomically high," though real labeled data is scarce and proprietary (a corpus challenge).

**10. RL for chip floorplanning / macro placement (Mirhoseini, Goldie et al., "A graph placement methodology for fast chip design," Nature 2021, [link](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03544-w))**
An RL agent (edge-based GCN) produces macro placements that are used in Google's TPU, generating layouts **in under 6 hours vs. weeks–months** of human effort at comparable/better PPA.
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** Collapses a weeks-long expert design loop into hours, and each placement iteration otherwise consumes scarce senior-engineer time. **Caveat — actively contested:** see the [AlphaChip controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlphaChip_(controversy)) and the "False Dawn" reevaluation (arXiv 2306.09633); present the speedup as claimed-and-disputed, not settled.
- **Learnings for FF4:** A cautionary tale on corpus/benchmark integrity — the dispute is precisely about evaluation setup and unreleased data. FF4's credibility hinges on transparent, reproducible test splits from the start.

**11. Data-center cooling optimization (DeepMind/Google, 2016, [blog](https://blog.google/outreach-initiatives/environment/deepmind-ai-reduces-energy-used-for/))**
Neural nets predict near-future temperature/pressure and PUE under proposed control actions, so the controller simulates recommendations before applying them to live cooling plant.
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** **40% reduction in cooling energy, ~15% reduction in overall PUE overhead**; fleet-wide rollout is estimated to save on the order of a few TWh/year (~$200–300M at industrial prices — an analyst extrapolation, not an official Google figure, so flag it).
- **Learnings for FF4:** Demonstrates the "predict outcome of a proposed action within safety constraints" loop — the WM here doubles as a guardrail, rejecting configs that would violate operating limits. FF4 should predict *both* the objective and constraint-violation states.

**12. Virtual screening / active-learning triage in drug & materials discovery (AI-accelerated docking, Nature Communications 2024, [link](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-52061-7))**
ML models triage billions of candidate molecules, predicting which few are worth expensive docking or wet-lab assays; active learning trains a target-specific net during the screen to prioritize compounds.
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** The discovery phase alone runs **~$430M and 5–6 years** (full approved-drug cost cited at $2–3B / >10 years); one platform reported **5–8% hit rates while assaying only ~85 compounds per target**. Value = wet-lab experiments avoided, each of which is slow and costly relative to a compute prediction.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Canonical triage economics — cheap predictor gating a hugely expensive physical experiment, with active learning closing the loop. Argues FF4 should support *sequential* prediction where each real run it does commission is chosen to maximize information.

**13. A/B experiment outcome prediction via surrogate metrics (Duan et al., WSDM 2021, arXiv 2106.01421; Microsoft "Toward Predicting the Outcome of an A/B Experiment for Search Relevance," [PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/paper-11.pdf))**
Predict a long-horizon "true-north" metric from short-term surrogates (or offline) so a variant's likely win/loss is estimated before running a full-length live experiment. Netflix reported ~95% consistency between 14-day surrogate predictions and 63-day outcomes across 1,098 arms.
- **Why prediction is valuable (quantified):** Experiment slots and user-traffic are the scarce resource; shortening a 63-day readout to 14 days (or screening offline) multiplies experiment throughput. The published caveat is real: surrogate-based tests carry elevated false-positive rates, so prediction augments rather than replaces the run.
- **Learnings for FF4:** Directly parallels our "predict the measured outcome before the expensive eval" thesis, and honestly quantifies the *failure mode* of surrogate prediction (false positives) — a template for how FF4 should report uncertainty rather than overclaim.

### Takeaways for FF4 (ranked by corpus strength)

1. **DL training-job failures (#4) are the single best value anchor and near-ideal corpus.** Real, labeled, published (ICSE'20 Distinguished Paper), and it puts a hard number on our v1 OOM domain (GPU-OOM = largest runtime-failure category; 8.8% of all failures). Lead with this to justify the whole project.
2. **CI predictive test selection (#3) and HPC queue/runtime (#6) offer the richest self-labeling corpora at scale.** Every build/job is a free labeled example (Google: ~150M test runs/day; 99% pass), so class-imbalanced rare-failure prediction — the exact FF4 skill — can be trained and evaluated at volume.
3. **Neural scaling laws (#2) are the cleanest structural template** for "structured config → measured scalar," and prove the market already trusts extrapolation from cheap runs to avoid multi-million-dollar ones.
4. **LLM routing (#1) and query-optimizer cost estimation (#8) show the decision-regret framing matters more than average error** — FF4 should be scored on whether it picks the right config, not on RMSE, and its confidence must be calibrated near decision boundaries.
5. **Tape-out/mask (#9) and drug screening (#12) are the extreme-value frontier** ($10M+ respins; ~$430M discovery phase) where even a weak WM pays for itself — but real labeled data is proprietary/scarce, making them aspirational corpora rather than near-term ones.
6. **Batfish (#7) and DeepMind cooling (#11) set the bar FF4 must clear: predict constraint *violations*, not just a number, and be honest about uncertainty** (see #13's false-positive caveat). Where formal/simulation tools already give certainty, FF4 competes only on heterogeneous breadth.

*Verification notes: FrugalGPT's 98% is dataset-specific (HEADLINES); AlphaChip's speedup is peer-reviewed but actively disputed; IBS tape-out figures are contested as ~50–66% high; spot-eviction "60-min horizon," DeepMind's ~$200–300M/yr, and enterprise "40–70% LLM savings" are vendor/analyst claims, not peer-reviewed — all flagged inline.*
## Quantifying the value of pre-run prediction, domain by domain

### 1. ML training memory/OOM + throughput

**Cost of one real measurement.** GPU rental sets the unit cost. H100 on-demand runs roughly **$1.49–$6.98/GPU-hr** across providers (2025–2026): Lambda lists H100 SXM at **$3.99–$4.29** and PCIe at **$3.29** (vendor-primary, https://lambda.ai/pricing); marketplace/secure clouds span RunPod ~$1.99–$2.39, Vast.ai ~$1.87, AWS P5 ~$3.90 (post-June-2025 cut), GCP A3 ~$3.00, Azure NC-H100-v5 ~$6.98, CoreWeave ~$6.16 (aggregator https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/h100-rental-prices-cloud-comparison, industry-survey). A single large training/finetune run costs hundreds-to-thousands of GPU-hours; per-attempt cost is real money and multi-hour-to-multi-day latency.

**How often the loop is wasted.** The Philly cluster trace ("Analysis of Large-Scale Multi-Tenant GPU Clusters for DNN Training Workloads," USENIX ATC 2019, Jeon et al.) reports **30.7% of jobs are terminated by users or fail, consuming ~55% of total GPU time** (https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc19/presentation/jeon, peer-reviewed). "An Empirical Study on Program Failures of Deep Learning Jobs" (ICSE 2020, Zhang et al., https://hongyujohn.github.io/icse20-main-199.pdf, peer-reviewed) classified 4,960 real Philly failures: **GPU out-of-memory is ~8.8% of all failures — the largest DL-specific crash category** — while execution-environment problems are the biggest overall bucket at 48%. The repeated "65% of failures are OOM" is **not defensible**: it conflates denominators; the clean number is 8.8% of all failures.

**Sweep sizes.** No canonical figure; published points put HPO at **~10²–10³ trials** (VTAB ~100, optimizer studies 200–412, RL guides ~250) and NAS at 10³+ (Vertex NAS docs cite ~2000 to converge). Any universal "average sweep size" is folklore.

**Value thesis:** ~55% of GPU-hours in a real multi-tenant cluster go to jobs that fail or get killed, and OOM is the top DL-specific crash — predicting OOM/throughput before submission reclaims wasted spend and multi-day latency.

### 2. LLM inference serving config

**Cost of one sweep — verified.** Vidur (MLSys 2024, Agrawal et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05465, peer-reviewed) states verbatim: *"Vidur-Search finds the best deployment configuration for LLaMA2-70B in one hour on a CPU machine, in contrast to a deployment-based exploration which would require 42K GPU hours - costing ~218K dollars."* The figure is **confirmed exactly** (implied basis ~$5.19/GPU-hr; search covers "hundreds of configurations").

**Search space.** Combinatorial over tensor-parallel × pipeline-parallel × replica count × batch/chunk-scheduling × hardware SKU. No peer-reviewed canonical count beyond Vidur's "hundreds" per (model, workload); any "thousands" claim is extrapolation.

**SLO-violation cost.** No clean dollar-per-violation figure is published. What exists is throughput-denominated: tight TTFT/TPOT SLAs cost **60–93% of throughput** vs relaxed settings (https://arxiv.org/html/2606.20577v1, preprint); under a 2× surge vLLM breached SLO by 24% (prefill)/60% (decode) latency (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.17133, preprint). The circulated "$13→$130/MTok at 10% utilization" is **an anecdote its own source says is unquantified** (https://arxiv.org/html/2606.11690) — do not cite as a hard number.

**Value thesis:** the single strongest datapoint in the taxonomy — an exhaustive real config search for one 70B model costs ~$218K/42K GPU-hours, and a simulator replaces it with one CPU-hour.

### 3. FPGA synthesis + place-and-route (and ASIC adjacency)

**Cost of one measurement (runtime).** P&R runs from **~30 min** for a mid-size, 50%-utilized ZU9EG (Vivado, "Reducing FPGA Compile Time with Separate Compilation," FPT 2019, https://ic.ese.upenn.edu/pdf/prflow_fpt2019.pdf, peer-reviewed), through **42–160 min** for un-optimized benchmark mappings, up to **~4–6 hrs per run** for very large (>80%-utilized) data-center FPGAs (EDABoard threads + Coyote v2 arXiv:2504.21538; mixed folklore/peer-reviewed). ASIC full-chip P&R at advanced nodes routinely runs overnight-to-multi-day per iteration, but no specific hour/day figure ties to a clean primary source — treat multi-day ASIC P&R as industry lore.

**Tool + iteration cost.** EDA seats are estimated at **~$50K–$500K per seat/year** (SemiAnalysis EDA primer, https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/eda-market-primer, vendor-blog/industry-survey) — no vendor publishes list prices. No rigorous study counts P&R iterations per design; only qualitative statements exist ("multiple iterations to timing closure add weeks or months," AnySilicon, vendor-blog). Precise iteration counts are untraceable.

**ASIC respin stakes.** Mask/photomask set costs (SemiEngineering "What Will That Chip Cost?", https://semiengineering.com/what-will-that-chip-cost/, news/industry): 28nm **>$1M**, 7nm **>$10M**, 3nm **~$40M**. Full design NRE (IBS via SemiEngineering/Tom's Hardware, industry-survey relayed by news): 7nm **$297.8M**, 5nm **$542.2M**, 3nm **$0.5–1.5B**, 2nm **~$725M** — though SemiAnalysis argues IBS runs 50–66% high. A respin "budget 50–100% of mask cost" rule traces only to forums (**folklore**).

**Value thesis:** iteration latency (hours per FPGA run; days per ASIC) and the catastrophic tail — a single advanced-node respin costs $1M–$40M in masks alone — make predicting timing closure/routability before the run enormously valuable (latency + respin tail-risk).

### 4. Build/CI resource profiling

**Scale of the loop.** "Taming Google-Scale Continuous Testing" / "State of CI Testing @Google" (ICSE-SEIP 2017, Memon et al., https://research.google/pubs/pub45861, peer-reviewed): **4.2M distinct tests, 150M test executions/day, ~35 runs/test/day.** Microsoft CloudBuild (FSE 2016, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/q_signed-2.pdf, peer-reviewed): **>20K builds/day on up to 10K machines**, 1.3–10× faster than prior systems. Google's "% of total compute spent on testing" does **not** trace to a primary source — its absence is flagged rather than invented.

**Published prediction savings.** Meta's "Predictive Test Selection" (ICSE-SEIP 2019, Machalica et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.05286, peer-reviewed) states verbatim it **cuts total testing infrastructure cost by a factor of two (~50%)** while catching **>95%** of individual test failures and **>99.9%** of faulty changes.

**Value thesis:** at 150M test executions/day, a learned predictor of which tests matter halves testing compute (Meta's measured 2×) with negligible miss rate — the one domain with a directly-measured prediction-savings number.

### 5. Media encoding (ffmpeg / Netflix)

**Scale and compute.** Video encoding is **~70% of Netflix compute, running across ~300,000 CPUs in >1000 autoscaling groups** ("High Quality Video Encoding at Scale," https://netflixtechblog.com/high-quality-video-encoding-at-scale-d159db052746 + InfoQ talk https://www.infoq.com/presentations/video-encoding-netflix/, vendor-blog). Per-title optimization brute-forces **hundreds of resolution/bitrate combinations** per title to find the convex hull, for ~20% bitrate savings (https://netflixtechblog.com/per-title-encode-optimization-7e99442b62a2); the shot-based Dynamic Optimizer adds ~30% average savings and processes ~900 shots per Stranger Things episode (https://netflixtechblog.com/dynamic-optimizer-a-perceptual-video-encoding-optimization-framework-e19f1e3a277f), increasing chunk count ">2 orders of magnitude per encode" (https://netflixtechblog.com/optimized-shot-based-encodes-now-streaming-4b9464204830). The often-cited **~190,000 CPU-hours per season** (Stranger Things S2) traces to the InfoQ talk, not a primary blog — treat as talk-derived. No public $/year total for the encoding farm exists.

**Value thesis:** the optimization itself is a giant per-title search (hundreds of encodes each, across 70% of a 300K-CPU fleet) — predicting the quality/bitrate curve without running every encode is a straight compute-cost win at massive scale.

### 6. Postgres / DB query performance

**Cost of a bad plan.** Leis et al., "How Good Are Query Optimizers, Really?" (PVLDB 9(3), 2015, https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol9/p204-leis.pdf, peer-reviewed): cardinality **underestimation up to 10⁸ and overestimation up to 10⁴** on intermediate joins; the fraction of estimates off by ≥10× grows **16%→32%→52%** from 1 to 3 joins in PostgreSQL; with FK indexes, **~40% of queries run ≥2× slower** using estimated vs true cardinalities. The cost model is a minor factor (tuning it gave only 34–41%), so bad **cardinality estimates → wrong join orders → orders-of-magnitude slowdowns** is the headline. A 2025 follow-up confirms the framing (PVLDB 18, http://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol18/p5531-viktor.pdf, peer-reviewed).

**Industry $ cost.** No credible, traceable primary "$X/year lost to slow queries" figure exists; circulating numbers are vendor marketing/folklore. Flagging the absence.

**Value thesis:** the ambiguity/robustness case — bad plans cause silent orders-of-magnitude slowdowns, and predicting execution cost/plan quality before running catches the tail estimators miss.

### 7. Power/thermal (GPU power capping, datacenter cooling)

**DeepMind cooling — verified with a nuance.** Google/DeepMind (July 20, 2016, https://blog.google/outreach-initiatives/environment/deepmind-ai-reduces-energy-used-for/, vendor-blog) claimed a reduction in cooling energy of **"up to 40 percent"** and **"a 15 percent reduction in overall PUE overhead."** The "40%" is *cooling* energy only and is "up to"; the facility-wide figure is the 15% PUE-overhead number. Secondary "$200–300M/yr" and "2–3 TWh" fleet-savings figures are **third-party extrapolations**, not in Google's post.

**GPU power-capping (peer-reviewed, consistent).** Zeus (NSDI '23, https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi23/presentation/you) reports **15.3–75.8% energy reduction** co-tuning batch size and power limit. DEPO (FGCS 2023, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167739X23001267) shows **~15–18% energy saved at ~8% perf loss**. A multi-GPU study (https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-48803-0_1) gets **≥25% energy improvement** capping 8 GPUs at 160–200W. Consistent takeaway: **capping ~20–40% below default yields ~10–25% energy savings for <10% throughput loss.**

**Value thesis:** perf-per-watt has a well-mapped Pareto frontier (10–25% energy for <10% throughput); predicting the throughput/power tradeoff lets you pick the operating point without sweeping — cost and (via cooling/thermal) safety.

### 8. Battery cycle-life

**Cost/time of one measurement.** Cycling a ~1,000-cycle cell to end-of-life (80% capacity) takes **~6 months** at 25°C/0.5C (~4.5 h/cycle × 1,000; vendor-blog https://www.huntkeyenergystorage.com/battery-life-cycle/, arithmetic sound); realistic multi-condition testing takes years, the stated motivation in accelerated-testing papers. No public per-channel cycler pricing exists (Arbin/Maccor/Neware quote-on-request); any specific $/channel is folklore.

**Published prediction speedups — verified, with a correction.** Severson et al. (Nature Energy 2019, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41560-019-0356-8, peer-reviewed): 124 LFP cells (150–2,300 cycle lives), **9.1% error predicting cycle life from the first 100 cycles**, **4.9% classification error from the first 5 cycles** — before meaningful degradation. Attia et al. (Nature 2020, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-1994-5, verified via https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32076218/, peer-reviewed) states verbatim: identified high-cycle-life protocols among **224 candidates in 16 days vs over 500 days by exhaustive search**. Note: this is a **>30× reduction (>97% time saved)** — the sometimes-quoted "~15×" cannot be traced to either paper.

**Value thesis:** the purest latency/ambiguity case — each measurement takes months, and early-prediction + closed-loop optimization compresses a >500-day search into 16 days (>30×), the second-strongest quantified prediction win in the taxonomy.

### 9. PCB respins / board bring-up

**Cost per respin — weak provenance, flagged.** Vendor pages give wildly inconsistent figures (a folklore red flag): **$10K–$50K per iteration** with a worked example of $86K (EMA, https://www.ema-eda.com/ema-resources/blog/cost-of-pcb-design-spins-emd, vendor-blog, explicitly "industry estimates," no citation); **$44K + 8.5 days** and separately "$3K–$15K" (Cadstrom, secondhand). Delay per respin ranges 8.5 days to ~4 weeks. The much-quoted **2.9 respins per project** traces to a **Lifecycle Insights (Sept 2018) report that is not directly retrievable** — effectively secondhand.

**Fraction needing respins.** The "**90% of prototypes fail first attempt**" claim cites a "2025 IPC Prototype Reliability Report" that **could not be verified to exist** — treat as folklore. More credible: Aberdeen Group (via Siemens) says **~50% of complex products need ≥1 additional design iteration**, and the 2025 PCD&F Salary Survey (real recurring trade survey) finds **~60% of designers produce 1–5 respins/year**.

**Value thesis:** predicting board-bring-up failures avoids fab + assembly cost and multi-week fabrication latency per spin — but honestly, PCB dollar figures are the **least trustworthy in this set** (EDA-vendor marketing, varying 3–4× across pages, circular sourcing).

### 10. 3D printing / CNC machining

**The one solid number.** "Causes of Desktop FDM Fabrication Failures in an Open Studio Environment" (Procedia CIRP 2018/2019, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2212827118312861, peer-reviewed) measured a **41.1% overall FDM failure rate, 26.3% attributable to human error**, in a Georgia Tech makerspace — and found experience did not reduce failures.

**Everything else is folklore.** Experience-tiered rates (15–25% beginners, 2–5% print farms), "training cuts failures 33%→6%", CNC scrap **3–8%**, "15–30% of metal becomes swarf", and the Toyota 20%→8% anecdote all come from blog aggregators/vendor pages with **no retrievable primary source** — illustrative only.

**Value thesis:** predicting print/machining failure saves material, machine-hours, and rework; the effect is real (~41% measured FDM failure) but the domain is thinly sourced beyond that one study.
